# SCHLELBERG

## Chapter Twelve — What the Records Keep

Mariya's messages reached Tobious after he had stopped expecting them to arrive together.

The two wrappers lay in a tray at the dispatch desk, beneath routine parts from Salt Gate and a notice about delayed medicine. Inspection marks covered one corner of the first. The second had been opened and resealed with a strip that bore the Concord's three-line sign. Whoever had examined it had not bothered to hide the fact.

The dispatcher brought out the route slips when Tobious asked why they had come together. One wrapper had waited at Salt for an outgoing service load. The other had reached the station on time but been held while a clerk compared its marks with an authorized supply manifest. The delay was written in ordinary terms and signed by people Tobious did not know. He wanted a single man to blame for keeping Mariya's words away from him. Instead he found a chain of small permissions and queues, each one acceptable by itself, together long enough for her first warning to age.

“Can we give her a faster route?” he asked Ypin.

“A diagnostic relay, if the Storehouse branch can spare power. Until then a person still has to carry this across Salt.”

Tobious folded the route slips with the wrappers. The future had not abolished distance. It had given distance clerks and reasons.

Tobious knew the scratch of a leaf at once. He had watched Mariya make it in the soft clay of his mother's jar when they were children and had no ink worth keeping. Below it stood seven strokes. On the reverse, four. He unfolded the linen carefully enough that Ypin, waiting beside him, lost patience and took the torn corner so it would not catch.

Mariya had written names in the marks she knew Tobious could recognize, with the porter translating a few into future script at the margin. She had traced the way the water trembled at the ninth step, the three clicks, the seam that opened when the guard crossed. A later line named the red-marked passage Lios had used with the water crew. Near its end she had made a small drawing of his repaired cup. It told Tobious the boy was alive in a way no official seal could.

“She sends a lot for one strip,” Ypin said.

“She had to.”

He found the line about Pehl, the potter. Pehl had repaired the handle of Tobious's mother's jar two harvests earlier, arguing that it was too well made to discard for one cracked fitting. Another name, Annet, belonged to a woman who had helped watch an injured goat when his parents were ill. He had not known she was working below. A village could make a person familiar without making him attentive. Mariya's marks corrected his memory.

“I need to go back,” Tobious said.

“You need to see what the manifests say first.”

“She saw them with her own eyes.”

“Yes. I want a record that makes your commanders answer for the same people.”

Tobious began to argue, then noticed Ypin's hands. He had already sorted the wrappers by their dispatch marks. He believed Mariya's count enough to search. He was asking for proof that could travel farther than belief.

They went to the service records. Ypin's access opened the maintenance layer, not the command layer. Each ancient site had a page for power, material flow, and biological load. A separate public page showed protected residents. Tobious could read only the translated headings, so Ypin held the display still while the words settled behind his ear.

At the Storehouse branch, the first column read **UNREGISTERED BIOLOGICAL LOAD: 58**.

The next read **PROTECTED PERSONS: 0**.

Tobious pointed to the zero. “Mariya sent names.”

“The first column doesn't say they aren't people,” Ypin said. “The second says none of them qualify for protection under this register.”

He opened a subpage. The loads had shift times and food allocations. One entry aligned with the day Pehl had received bread from Mariya. Its ordinary service label carried his physical identifier in a field hidden from the public tally.

“That's him,” Tobious said.

Ypin did not answer at once. He checked the identifier against another page and found the same shift. “It is consistent with him. I want an independent match.”

“How many matches until you call him a person?”

Ypin flinched. Tobious knew the question was unfair even while he spoke it. The technician had not made the column. He had helped maintain it without knowing who might be in it; that was the part both of them were afraid to name.

“One,” Ypin said. “For me. More for the board that will say I changed a number after I lost my head.”

They retrieved two more records. A ration dispatch to the annex showed fifty-eight portions. A repair request mentioned a living stabilizer shift beneath the painted seam. It did not use a human name. The records were precise where precision kept the system running and vague where it might invite a question.

Tobious asked for a copy. Ypin gave him an authenticated excerpt and kept the access trail. He could see that someone else had opened the same pages before them, but not who; the command layer held that detail behind another permission.

They brought the excerpt to Edda before meeting Panjios. She compared the fifty-eight biological loads with the ration record and said she could open a preliminary patient inquiry, but could not certify injuries without a way to examine the people or their direct accounts. Tobious put Mariya's names beside the column. Edda copied them carefully, leaving blank spaces where Mariya had not been able to confirm a person under guard. “Do not fill those with guesses to make the form look finished,” she said. “A blank here tells the next person where to ask.”

Ypin agreed to preserve the service trail while Tobious prepared to cross. It was the first piece of the investigation that could survive if the field visit failed. Tobious wished the protection of a copy felt more like protection for the people themselves. Edda did not pretend it did.

“Mariya is near fifty-eight people your count refuses to see,” Tobious said. “We need to know how many of them can leave.”

“I agree.”

“Then help me get there.”

“I will.” Ypin closed the display. “But don't mistake a number that proves they exist for a map of what happens if you move them.”

Tobious looked again at the zero beside *protected persons*. It was a kind of blindness chosen carefully enough to have columns.

***

Panjios met them in the calibration gallery. He had been reviewing the Storehouse anomaly from the day Tobious first touched the frame. Tobious had not told him every detail of what he had seen. He had feared that a man who understood the root might tell him his bleeding future was fixed beyond his choosing. The new records made withholding it feel like another way to stay ignorant.

“The person in the first image was me,” he said.

Panjios set down his stylus. “You recognized his voice?”

“And his face. He had a scar here.” Tobious touched below his left eye. “He was holding his right ribs. There was a road above him, and it was falling.”

Ypin turned the diagnostic record toward them. It showed a short flare at the moment of acquisition, forty-one seconds long. No street appeared in its saved data, only a disturbance in the return line and a trace that ended as abruptly as it began.

“That's all?” Tobious asked.

“The root recorded that a signal crossed its own history,” Panjios said. “It did not record what you saw.”

“Can we make it happen again?”

“No. It was the first reference contact. There is no second first contact to choose.”

Panjios showed them a model of the paired line, with one end at Tobious's acquisition and another near a terminal disturbance not yet dated in the current forecasts. The latter was only a range of possible load states. It was not a calendar he could use to avoid a fall.

“A damaged root can carry a sensory trace from its closing end to its beginning,” Panjios said. “One brief trace. Air and matter do not go with it.”

“He spoke to me.”

“Then he spoke in the event that produced the trace.”

“Could I tell him something now? Could he tell me how to stop it?”

“There is no open channel to him. The trace has happened. It was not a door you could enter when you touched it.”

Tobious remembered the rod striking the image of the street. He had learned that with his own painful hand. Yet he had kept a second hope hidden behind the first: that if the man was him, he might one day choose better words and send them back to save everyone.

“If he was me,” he said, “then the road will fall.”

“You saw a road falling,” Panjios said. “You did not see who lived beneath it, or what became of you after you spoke. A glimpse does not tell you which choice made the scene, only that it belongs to this history.”

Tobious looked at the trace. He had seen the man bleeding but still standing. He could not use those forty-one seconds as proof that Mariya would live through the next hour, much less the next weeks. The image was real. It was not permission to stop asking what his decisions would cost.

Panjios saw the wrappers in his hand. “You want a field visit.”

“I want to see whether those fifty-eight are the people she named.”

“We need to map the load before we remove anyone.”

“You already have the numbers.”

“We have a count. Not the distribution.”

Tobious heard the distinction. He hated it because it was exactly the kind of thing a man might say to keep a person waiting. It was also the reason he had stopped turning the powered valve when Ypin told him he would break it. He did not yet know which use Panjios was making of the truth.

***

Suss received them in the operations room on Day 40. The city behind the glass wall was bright with morning, though one service tier was shaded dark for repairs. He had read the excerpt and Mariya's names before they entered.

“An unregistered load can contain people,” he said. “The category is not a declaration that they are stone.”

“Then why does the protected column say zero?” Tobious asked.

“Because the compact defines who we can move under civil priority. The ancient territories were not registered with it.”

“They live beside your receiver.”

“Which is why I will send a team.”

Suss placed a field map on the table. The Storehouse branch had been active more often than the public schedule showed. The main chamber was stable only during a short Day-42 window. A repair team could enter through Salt Gate, inspect the annex, and test the damaged component. Tobious would come as the living reference. Panjios would supervise field safety. Ypin would record the human tally. Suss would direct the operation from Veyr, with a route officer listening at Salt.

“An inspection,” Tobious said.

“An inspection and a repair. If we know who is inside, we can plan movement without collapsing their support.”

“Mariya has been counting them for weeks.”

“She has counted people. I need to know what the receiver is doing to them.”

Suss's answer sounded like the one Ypin had given him at the records desk. It could be care, or it could be a reason for leaving a dangerous arrangement intact. Tobious had seen Suss spend a shield to rescue workers whose names he had not known. That memory made it harder, not easier, to decide what the commander intended here.

“What if one of them is in danger while we inspect?” Tobious asked.

“You can stabilize the branch under Panjios's direction. You cannot cut a live load line until we map where its strain will go.”

Suss looked directly at him. “Your first response is to pull someone out. Sometimes that is right. At the Storehouse, a wrong cut could put the cost on the people you do not see.”

Tobious wanted to say that a system which hid them had made seeing difficult on purpose. He would say it when they had reached the annex. For now Suss was giving him a route home and a chance to measure what Mariya had found. He accepted the limits aloud.

After the meeting, Ypin walked with him to the service lift.

“You sounded as though you believed him,” Ypin said.

“I believe the cut can hurt people I can't see.”

“And the rest?”

Tobious looked at the map in his hand. The three ancient sites were marked there, Aras no larger than a fingernail. He knew Mariya had gone below with food and come back. The next time a guard threatened someone there, he did not know whether he could stand beside her and leave a person to the system's care.

“The rest,” he said, “we find out when we get there.”

He had agreed to an inspection. He intended to keep it an inspection until someone he could name was harmed in front of him. It was a private exception to a spoken promise. He did not tell Ypin or Panjios, because he knew what they would say. The silence was a choice before the cut ever happened.
