# SCHLELBERG

## Chapter Ten — The Ninth Step

Mariya gained permission to enter the lower worksite by telling Darran that his people were eating from the wrong count.

She brought no accusation to the old storehouse. She brought a board of rations, its grooves worn deep by twenty-one days of revisions, and three small loaves she could show were missing from the delivery. Darran stood beneath the empty place where the woman-with-bowl statue had once rested. He wore a clean tunic, though the men coming up from the stairs had salt on their skin and gray dust in their hair.

“Your scribe writes a portion for every person assigned below,” she said. “The kitchen sends a portion for every name it has. Those aren't the same people.”

“The kitchen is your concern.”

“The workers fainting under your stone are yours.”

He studied her board. She had been careful to include genuine shortages from the mill and not only the names hidden from the tablet. A lie would be easier for him to dismiss than a problem that cost him labor.

“You can reconcile the meals,” he said. “A guard goes with you. You touch no machinery.”

Mariya almost asked whether the machinery could account for its own meals. She held her tongue. She wanted to reach the people who went through Lios's red-marked door.

The lower passage had changed since Tobious left. Wooden props braced the old walls. A new rail kept carts from striking the black frame. Thin lines of pale material had been fitted between stones that did not match them. Their glow was intermittent, like a breath taken under a blanket.

At the foot of the first stair stood baskets of crushed shell used to keep the damp floor from becoming slick. A village mason had shown Darran's crew where the old vault could bear a cart; someone had repaid him by adding his name to a longer labor shift. Mariya recognized his wedge marks in the new props. The occupation had not built this place from nothing. It had used the knowledge of people who would later be told the works did not belong to them.

The guard brought her to a ration hatch built into the annex beyond the circular chamber. He kept one hand on his spear. Behind the hatch she found people stacking cut stone, sorting the pieces by marks Darran had given them. Another group cleared silt from a channel. The work had purpose; that made it more dangerous than mere punishment. Whoever wanted the old threshold had planned how to uncover more of it.

Thesia came first, asking for a sole to be tied back onto her sandal. “If I go down with a foot open, it will split,” she said. She showed Mariya where the leather had parted. There was dried blood along the edge.

She had wrapped the broken sole in twine cut from a grain sack. Mariya offered to take her off the stone shift until it could be repaired. Thesia shook her head. “If I leave, they put my sister's eldest in my place. He still trips over his own feet.” It was not pride in endurance. It was a choice made inside a rota Darran controlled. Mariya wrote the child's name beside the repair request so Darran could not solve one shortage by creating another.

Mariya added the repair to her board and gave her a strip of linen from the bundle she had carried in. The guard began to object, then saw Darran watching the cart work and let it pass.

Pehl, a broad-backed potter who made the jars for the common store, wanted a share of food kept aside for his child. “My daughter brings water at dusk,” he said. “She won't eat until I do.”

“What is her name?” Mariya asked.

“You know her. She spilled dye on the threshing floor last spring.”

“I know. I want it written here.”

He watched her carve the marks. The board could hold a line for a child whom the palace tablet did not count. It could not get her father out.

Annet complained that the water skins smelled stale. She spoke quickly, with the sharpness of someone who had asked twice and been told the smell was harmless. Hessah took one of the skins, turned its mouth inside out, and found a seam leaking against the stopper. She retied it with a strip torn from her own hem, then gave it to Annet to test.

“I know the taste of bad water,” Annet told the guard before drinking. “I do not need your permission to notice it.”

The guard said the kitchen had delivered what it was ordered to deliver. He was not lying about the order. He had still watched them drink from the skin. Mariya made him hold the vessel while Hessah checked the rest. Two more mouths were damp. They would need clean strips and time to dry, neither listed on Darran's ration board. She added them there while he watched.

“The water is old,” Hessah said. “The skin was making it worse.”

Annet drank and passed it to a woman behind her. Mariya recorded both the complaint and the repair. If she wrote only the complaint, Darran could claim the vessel had been sound. If she wrote only the repair, the people who had drunk from it would vanish from the account.

The guard counted ten workers at the hatch. Mariya saw twelve. One had gone behind a baffle before he arrived, another knelt near a drain and was hidden from the passage. She kept her face on the board. When she reached the end of her ration marks, she had names for eleven. The twelfth had refused to say his while a Telassari guard stood beside him. She left a blank line rather than make one up.

Darran came to the hatch as she was closing it.

“You didn't need to speak to each one,” he said.

“I need to know who eats.”

“My man can give you a total.”

“Your man gave me ten.” She turned the board. “You have twelve.”

Darran's gaze moved past her into the annex. “Two are on a different shift.”

“They are here now.”

For a moment his face lost the surveyor's patience. Mariya saw that she had taken a measurement he had preferred to keep fluid. Then he gestured toward the stairs.

“Finish before the next baffle cycle.”

***

The ninth step was marked by a crack running through its center. Lios had told her to notice it. Water gathered in the crack, though the floor around it stayed nearly dry.

Mariya paused there as the guard went ahead. In a shallow bowl abandoned on a ledge, the surface trembled. She thought someone was walking on the upper stair. The sound came instead from inside the wall: a sequence of clicks, one high, two low. A pale seam opened across the passage a hand's width ahead of the guard. He stopped. After the third click, he crossed.

She watched where he put his feet. The seam had already begun to fade.

“Wait,” Darran said from below.

She was halfway through moving. The guard caught her shoulder and pulled her back. A line of cold air passed over the toe of her sandal. The seam reappeared exactly where she would have stepped.

“Three clicks,” she said.

“Three before the crossing,” Darran answered. “Not three after the last opening.”

Her mistake had been reasonable and would have hurt her. She wanted to be angry at him for letting her approach it. He had called out before she fell. She would have to use what he had shown her.

The bowl trembled again. This time she counted the clicks from the first one, not from the guard's movement. The seam held long enough for a cart to pass and then withdrew into the stone. The guard crossed. Mariya waited until Darran told her to follow.

On the other side, she looked back. The crack in the ninth step had caught a dark fleck that moved when the wall clicked, though the water did not otherwise flow. A person with a bowl could watch that fleck and predict the cycle's beginning. They might also confuse a delayed pulse for a safe one, as she had nearly done.

“Who taught the guards the count?” she asked.

“People who understand the structure.”

“Your men?”

“Enough questions.”

He led her to a storage niche where new fittings lay wrapped in cloth. She could tell they had not been made by any smith in Aras. The metal held a blue tint at its edge. An instruction sheet bore the same three-line mark she had seen on Panjios's case. Darran folded it before she could study the drawing.

Mariya checked the meal sacks, found another two portions undelivered, and made the guard carry them to the annex. She had earned entry by offering to reconcile food. She would not leave the people hungry merely because the wall had given her a more interesting problem.

Outside, she sat beneath the shade of the displaced statue until her pulse settled. She drew nine short lines on her board for the stairs, then tapped the ninth. She marked the bowl, the three clicks, the guard's wait, and the seam. The marks did not make her understand the device. They made her able to describe exactly where her understanding had failed.

That was a beginning.

***

At the coastal store, the carrier woman from the next inlet waited with a cart of barley wrapped beneath baskets of salt fish. The fig traders had carried it past the ridge in three small loads. None would appear impressive to Therren's men. Together they would let the lower quarter make bread for several more days.

“The toll is higher again,” the woman said as Mariya checked the sacks. “If we keep coming openly, the captain will take the cart.”

“There is a path from the north reed beds.”

“Only in dry weather. After rain the wheels sink.”

“Then we need a house on this side to receive it before the road. And another place for anyone hurt going through.”

The carrier named an old fisherman whose nets were stored in a covered yard. He would shelter a load if it did not endanger his grandchildren. Mariya asked to speak to him herself. A shelter was only real if the person inside it knew what had been promised in his name.

They moved the grain in ordinary baskets. The work made Mariya's shoulders ache. It was good to be tired from something that had arrived, rather than from a plan she could not yet prove.

Pehl's daughter came down to the store that evening to collect her father's share. Mariya had put his bread aside as he asked. The girl took it but did not leave.

“He said he would be on the day shift,” she said. “The guard says night.”

“I saw him today.”

“Then why can't he come home?”

Mariya looked at the wrapped loaf. The girl would carry it up to the site and might be turned away at the gate. Mariya had no answer that would make the arrangement just. She told her which guard had allowed food through, and what hour to try. The girl nodded with the seriousness of a person given a route instead of comfort.

Later, Lios carried a sealed bundle from the mill to the fisherman's yard. It was a short walk through familiar lanes; Mariya had asked his aunt first, and his aunt had walked the route with him once. The bundle held a ration tally, not a secret device or an order to approach the Receiver. When he returned, his repaired cup was tied at his waist.

“He had room for it,” Lios said. “The fisherman. He says one stretcher can fit by the nets if we move the hooks.”

“Did you move them?”

“No. His granddaughter did. She said I'd put them where people would step on them.”

Mariya smiled. The network was growing through people who could correct one another.

At the granary, she prepared a second marked wrapper for Tobious. She scratched the leaf, then drew the ninth step and the three clicks in marks she knew he would recognize as a water pattern. She wrote the names she had confirmed at the hatch. She left a space for the person who would not speak under guard.

The porter checked its size and tucked it into his supply case. This time he asked no question about what could be said. Mariya watched him carry it toward Salt Gate.

She had passed through the wall and returned with more names than the tablet had held. Darran had seen her count them. Tomorrow he might close the worksite to her. The door would remain, and the people behind it would still need food. She began thinking of another way in before the porter was out of sight.
