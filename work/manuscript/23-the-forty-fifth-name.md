# SCHLELBERG

## Chapter Twenty-Three — The Forty-Fifth Name

Nerissa read the roll while Mariya listened.

The first people answered quickly, relieved that they could. Farther down the list, voices came from the dry recess, the water line, the resting mats. One man lifted a hand because his throat had been raw since the last surge. Another answered for a woman who could not wake fully until he touched her shoulder. Nerissa waited for her own sign from each person instead of accepting the louder neighbor's word.

Mariya had made the list. For weeks she had read it herself, believing she could hear every omission before anyone else. Nerissa found two names whose marks had been transposed. Both people were alive. The correction felt like a rebuke and a relief.

When they came to the blank left where Pehl's name had been, his former work partner asked them not to close the space yet. “His daughter has not heard it from us,” he said. Nerissa said the blank would stay on the casualty tablet, but the living roll needed its own clear order. The man agreed after Mariya promised that the next carrier would take a spoken account as well as the written mark. He did not ask them to pretend Pehl might answer. He wanted the record to reach the person who would most need it.

Hessah was near the end. She raised her hand from the rest area, then asked if that counted as an answer. Her voice still carried its dry humor, but standing made the skin around her mouth go white. She had spent many days repairing water skins with a strip torn from her hem. Now she could barely hold the needle.

“We need to test how you will be carried,” Nerissa said. “May I examine your legs?”

Hessah nodded. She wanted her mother beside her, but she did not want her mother bearing the weight. She asked for the repaired water skin to stay with them. Its mouth had a red knot she could find by touch in the dark.

“It leaks less than the new ones,” she said.

“I believe you,” Mariya answered.

They had forty-five people, including Mariya. The number did not tell them that only thirty-one could walk the passage without help, or that four of those needed a hand at every turn. It did not tell them which children would freeze when the Iwang clicked or who could lift a stretcher after two nights without sleep. They began putting those answers beside the names, with each person's knowledge and consent where they could get it.

The first proposed order put Hessah near the front. A man whose ankle had been injured during the surge objected that he could still walk with a stick and would be easier to move while the passage was clear. The carriers wanted to take both in the first group. Nerissa showed them the turn: two supported people could not pass the wide recess at once while a third waited under the active baffle. The man suggested he go later, but Hessah said he should not make a noble offer while his ankle was swelling. They tested his pace. He could manage a short distance, rest, then go again. He chose the second walking group with someone beside him. Hessah kept the stretcher place, not because her life weighed more, but because this route could bear her best there.

Hessah's mother wanted to carry her daughter herself. She was broad-shouldered and had carried water for their household all her life. Hessah watched her demonstrate a lift with an empty basket and then shook her head.

“You know how to carry me from bed to the wash place,” she said. “That stair is not our house. You'll have to watch the field for both of us.”

Her mother looked wounded by the refusal. Mariya knew the feeling. To help a loved person could seem like the one right that disaster should not take away. She asked Hessah whom she would trust under a stretcher. Hessah chose two of the younger carriers who had practiced together. She asked her mother to walk where she could speak to her, not where she would have to bear her weight.

Nerissa recorded the choice. When Hessah grew tired and could not answer a question, her mother would not be asked to guess whether she had changed her mind without a reason. Mariya realized how often her earlier plans had said simply *move the weak* and left no place for a person to say who would touch them.

They tried the same question with others. One older man wanted his nephew beside him but said the nephew must not leave his wife alone at the far floor; a neighbor took that place. A woman who could walk if allowed to pause refused a stretcher that would keep her flat, where the old ceiling seemed to press down on her. They marked rest points for her instead. Each answer made the route more complicated. It also made it a route for these people rather than for forty-five interchangeable bodies.

Orran wanted to test the service recess. He had argued for sending strong people first. After Pehl and Annet died, he had stopped calling anyone weak when he meant frightened or ill. He came to Mariya with an offer that had a task attached.

“The lower brace can take a load if we wedge it before a release,” he said. “I'll hold it while the stretchers turn.”

“It may be the first place the field shifts,” she said.

“I know. Show me where I'd stand. I want to know whether I can get away if it fails.”

She did not praise him for volunteering. She showed him the sketch from the broken rope test and asked who would take over after one interval. Orran named two other stoneworkers. They tried the stance with an empty frame and found one could brace without blocking the path. If the field rose through the floor, they would abandon the brace and move back, even if that meant stopping a stretcher. Mariya wrote the limit down. He agreed to it.

The first route failed when they tried to turn the empty stretcher through a narrow passage. Its far handle struck the wall. A person lying on it would have scraped the active seam. Orran tried to angle it, then stopped before he forced it. They measured the wider recess again. It took longer to reach the safe stair and exposed the bearers to one extra baffle cycle, but it let a stretcher turn without bending the patient.

One of the proposed bearers objected to the wider route. The old wall there had held Thesia's broken ledge, and he had watched her fall. Orran made him put a hand on the new brace and feel which stones carried its weight. It was not a guarantee. The cracked lip that had failed Thesia was beyond this turn. Mariya asked the bearer whether he would work the route after seeing it, not after being ordered to trust Orran. He agreed to one rehearsal. If the brace moved under his hand, he would call a stop and leave the stretcher on stable floor until it could be reassessed.

They rehearsed a full turn without a patient. Lios called the baffle count from the recess, too softly for the bearers to hear. He tried again, louder, and the guard at the hatch turned his head. Mariya stopped the exercise before they drew attention to it. They changed the signal to the raised cup they had already practiced. Nerissa would repeat it from the near side; nobody would shout across the active field. A small failure in a harmless rehearsal had saved them from making that mistake while carrying Hessah.

Afterward Lios asked whether they could paint the cup rim so a bearer would see it when the light shifted. They had no paint the guard would ignore. Annet had left a strip of dark cloth among the water skins. Hessah tied a short piece to the handle, where it moved when Lios lifted the cup but did not obscure its shape. They tried the signal from both ends of the recess. The adult backup could see it. Lios touched the knot once, remembering whose cloth it had been, and put the cup beneath the shelf until the next bell.

“Longer, then,” Mariya said.

“Longer with a person still on it,” Orran replied.

He returned the frame to Hessah's resting place. She asked the two proposed bearers to lift it while she lay on it for a single turn, so she could tell them where the jolts hurt. Nerissa objected that the test would exhaust her. Hessah said she would rather find the painful corner now, before a field was rising at their feet. They agreed to one lift. Hessah was right about the second corner. Mariya marked it on the route.

***

Darran's ration schedule gave them a gap they could predict. On the fourth bell of each cycle, his guard left the inner hatch to collect a new tray. The hatch itself remained locked. The interval would not free anyone, but it would let them inspect the service panel's lower vent without a guard's eyes on their hands.

Mariya brought the idea to the distribution council before she asked Lios to watch the bell. One household representative said tampering with the panel would give Darran a reason to cut rations again if he found it disturbed. Orran said learning nothing about it would cost them a possible route for a stretcher. Both were making a claim about people who could not move quickly enough to hide from the consequences. Nerissa asked what they could learn through the vent now that could not wait until the outside team came.

“We can find the latch shape and sound the space behind it with a cord,” Orran said. “We cannot open it without a tool. I do not want to discover on the day of release that the opening is narrower than the frame.”

The representative asked that the cord be withdrawn and the vent left unmarked, and that a guard's approach end the test even if the work was unfinished. Mariya added a second person at the hatch to hear footsteps while Orran worked. They agreed. The decision did not make Darran harmless. It made the risk belong to the people who might pay for it.

Lios could hear the bell from the dry recess. Mariya offered him a task no adult needed to pretend was a heroic mission: raise his cup when the fourth bell sounded, then move away from the panel. Nerissa would watch the same bell from a second position if he was asleep, afraid, or unable to hear it. Lios practiced once and then asked whether he could refuse if the Iwang came close.

“Yes,” Mariya said. “The signal matters. You matter more.”

He considered this. “If I go to my aunt, tell her I didn't break this cup.”

“You helped make it useful.”

He turned it over in his hands, inspecting the smooth repaired rim.

On the following fourth bell Orran slipped a cord through the vent. He counted the knots that passed before its weighted end struck the inner wall. The space was deep enough for the stretcher's length but he could not tell how the door swung. He drew the cord back without scraping the edge. Mariya marked the unknown direction on her sketch. The test had answered one question and left another that mattered just as much: a room could be wide enough and still useless if its door opened into the patient. They would need a way to study the latch before the rescue.

At the next powered diagnostic poll, the wafer lit. Mariya recorded the forty-five names in the order Nerissa had read them. She named Thesia, Pehl, and Annet as the three dead since the entrapment. She added Hessah's condition, the number who needed carrying, the width of the stretcher route, the baffle intervals, and the ration gap. The stored Day-56 surge warning followed. She described the double click and the pressure across bodies before the ordinary seam opened.

She sent the packet. A mark showed the relay had received it. A later, smaller mark showed it had reached the Veyr dispatch side. There was no reply explaining what Tobious would do or when. She told the others exactly what the two marks meant.

Nerissa stayed beside her after they returned to the rest area. “You have stopped giving them a day you cannot know,” she said.

“I wanted the day to be true.”

“So did they.”

Mariya looked at the empty stretcher. The wider route was ready enough to describe. It was not safe until someone could release the field and keep the Iwang from using the seam. The outside team would need to hear how much of the plan belonged to people inside, and how much they still had to invent.

“I'm frightened,” she said.

Nerissa did not give her an assignment to cover the confession. She sat beside her and shared the last of a cup of water. Mariya accepted it. For the first time in days, she let someone else read the roll at night while she slept.
