# SCHLELBERG

## Chapter Twenty — A Name for the Missing

Pehl asked for the last clean cup because his daughter would be waiting at the outer hatch.

Mariya gave it to him. The cup had belonged to Lios's broken jug, and the boy had spent the morning carrying it between the water jars and the injured. Pehl wanted to fill it with the freshest water they had. The guard would probably make his daughter take it back empty, but he said the girl would know he had thought of her.

He had made a mark on the clay near the base, the shape his daughter used when she pressed a thumb into new pots. He had taught her to test a fired vessel with a small stone. The clear note meant it might hold water; the dull one meant a hidden crack. He tapped the cup now. Its repaired rim gave a note neither of them liked.

“It holds,” Lios said. He had watched the cup survive too many trips across the room to hear it judged by sound alone.

“You are right,” Pehl told him. “A potter can learn from a boy who carries the thing.”

Lios took the compliment seriously. He went to refill the other vessels, careful not to strike the repaired rim against the stone.

“Tell her you need new boots too,” Annet said. “She's been asking how to help.”

“I need a new stone wheel,” Pehl answered. “And a world where she has time to play instead of bringing food through a gate.”

Annet gave him a tired smile. Her shoulder from the guard's blow had healed badly. She could still tie the water skins, though she no longer carried full ones. She and Hessah had spent the night mending the worst seams with strips from their hems.

They had argued over whether the knot should be doubled. Annet liked the tighter join; Hessah said it wasted cloth and made the mouth hard to clean. At dawn Annet had tested both with a full skin. Hessah's held. Annet had used the other one anyway for the trip between jars, because its opening suited her sore shoulder. They were still debating it when Pehl asked for the cup. Mariya had been glad to hear two people quarrel about a repair instead of about who would be allowed to leave first.

Mariya watched them in the dry recess. Forty-seven people. She had begun to know the count by gaps in their work, not by the tile on which Nerissa wrote their names. Pehl always kept a small stone in his pocket to test the sound of a pot before firing it. Annet complained when the water was stale and then drank last. If either failed to answer, the room would change shape around the absence.

The pulse came while Pehl was lifting the cup.

The argument stopped in the middle of a word. Mariya saw Annet look up, recognizing a changed sound before she did. The basin water drew one way and then held, a slanted surface that should have fallen. Mariya shouted for everyone to leave the painted line. She had time to see Lios back away from it. Then the pressure took her balance.

Water rose against the side of the basin without a hand touching it. The baffle clicked twice too quickly, then made a low sound Mariya had never heard. People staggered. A line of cold pressure ran along the floor and tightened around their ankles. Mariya lost control of her own left hand for a moment; the cup she had been carrying fell and rolled beneath a shelf.

Pehl went down first. He struck the stone on his side, still trying to keep Lios's cup upright. Annet reached for him and could not coordinate her fingers. Hessah braced her against the wall. Nerissa called for everyone to stay off the painted seam until the pressure eased.

Mariya crawled to Pehl. His breath came in small, uneven pulls. He looked past her toward the outer hatch.

“The cup,” he said.

“It's here.”

She held it where he could see it. He seemed to want to say more, but the next breath did not complete. Nerissa reached them, checked for a pulse, then rested a hand on Mariya's wrist. Pehl's daughter would receive a cup of water and a name spoken by people who had watched him die. Mariya wanted the gift to be larger. It would not be.

The field released a little at a time. Annet could speak again. She asked whether Pehl had gone through the hatch. When Mariya told her, she turned her face to the wall and asked Hessah to leave the water skin beside her.

The surge left Annet unable to keep down the thin broth Darran's guards supplied. Nerissa treated her as best she could with water, rest, and the medicine they had saved. Annet could still complain about the stale taste on her tongue. She asked Hessah to show the others how to retie the skins so no one would have to drink from a leaking mouth again.

Before the last light went, she asked Mariya whether Pehl's daughter had been told. Mariya said she could send a message at the next window. Annet shook her head. The next window was a machine's chance, not a child's. She asked the guard at the hatch to pass a word to the girl when she came with the morning water. The guard refused to carry messages. Hessah told him the girl might bring food his shift needed; he could at least tell her whether to wait. The guard said he would speak if he saw her. No one knew whether he meant it.

Nerissa tried small sips rather than another bowl of broth. Annet kept one down and said the water still tasted stale. Hessah opened the skin she had mended with the looser knot and brought a fresh measure from the last clean jar. Annet noticed the knot and tried to argue. She could not finish the sentence. Hessah sat beside her and did not tell her to save her breath. Annet died before the next ration. Mariya heard Nerissa stop counting her pulse and begin the slower work of covering her body.

Mariya wrote both names on the tile. Pehl had died during the pulse. Annet had lived through it and died under the strain afterward. Collapsing the two into a single loss would make it easier for Darran to say the surge had ended when its warning lights went out.

***

Darran called it a stabilization event.

He spoke through the ration hatch while his guard set down fewer portions than the forty-five living people required. Mariya counted them twice. Darran said the root had drawn heavily and deliveries would resume after the line recovered. The food was being kept for a later shift, he said, not withheld from her group.

“There are only forty-five of us now,” she answered. “You brought food for thirty-eight.”

“Then divide it.”

She felt herself preparing to do exactly that, to choose who could bear hunger and then explain the choice after everyone had eaten. Nerissa stood beside her, waiting. Orran asked for a moment to speak with her in the sleeping recess.

“If we use the landing,” he said quietly, “we move the ones who can climb first. We don't tell the others until we know the route holds. Some will slow us before we have proof.”

“The tether stops us,” Mariya said.

“If Tobious cuts it. When he does. We should already know the order.”

“We should. We won't make that order in secret.”

“They'll refuse to be last.”

“They may. They live with the result.”

Orran looked away. She did not call him cruel. He had caught her belt when Thesia fell. He knew a weak ledge could take anyone. His proposal came from fear and a stoneworker's habit of putting the strongest support first. The weakness was treating people as timber that could be positioned without a word.

Nerissa called everyone who could move to the center of the dry floor. Those who could not were represented by the people caring for them, and Mariya made sure they could hear the discussion. She told them the actual ration number and said she could not promise the next delivery. She showed the storage vessels and asked how much water they should keep for the injured. Others offered answers she had not considered.

Hessah wanted water kept near the warm wall because the cold floor made her mother's joints seize when she stood to fetch it at night. A younger worker said the warm wall was closer to the active seam and the jars might break in another pulse. They moved the vessels one pace back and put two smaller skins within reach of those who could not walk. It was a compromise measured in arm lengths, not a principle that solved hunger. One man said he would forgo his portion. Nerissa refused to build the plan on people competing to be the most self-denying. She asked who had eaten last, who was ill, and who would need strength to carry water in the morning.

Then Nerissa said, “There is something else you owe them.”

Mariya knew. The protected seed jar sat above them in Aras, its broken seal a private mark she had made fifty days ago.

“Before we were trapped, I took grain from one of the two seed jars,” she said. “I fed households that had run out, including children here. I recorded how much. I did not ask every household that had a share in the jar.”

Annet's sister was not there; Pehl's daughter was outside. Among the captives, Hessah's mother said she had eaten that bread and would have asked for it herself. Another woman said the sowing would need every grain left and that Mariya had hidden the cost until it could no longer be challenged. Both were right about parts of what had happened.

“I would feed them again,” Mariya said. “I should have told you what I used and let you argue with me while there was time.”

“Will you ask next time?” Orran asked.

“Yes.”

He studied her, perhaps measuring whether the answer would survive urgency. She could not prove it with a speech. They formed a small distribution council: Nerissa for care, Hessah for water, Orran for the route and physical work, Mariya for the current stores and outside messages, and two people chosen by the other households. The council divided the thirty-eight portions and recorded who needed more the next day. The process did not create seven missing meals. It made the loss a shared decision instead of Mariya's private burden.

The first division took longer than Mariya would have taken alone. Two of the people who could walk objected to being given less than a full portion when they were assigned to lift jars. Hessah agreed they needed enough for that work, but asked why her mother had been left off the carrying list when she had been hauling water since before they were trapped. Orran changed the list. Nerissa found an ill man who had been counted as able to walk because he had done so once in the morning. They adjusted his portion and his task. No one applauded the council. The people ate, knowing who had made each choice and where to argue tomorrow.

***

At the next diagnostic window, the wafer refused to send.

Mariya had recorded the surge's timing, the two deaths, the current count of forty-five, and Darran's reduced rations. She added that the wall had made a new double click and that the pressure had reached bodies before the usual baffle opened. When she pressed the marked edge, the wafer showed a waiting light, then a broken one. It kept the message in memory. It had not handed it to the relay.

Lios watched from the dry recess. “Will Tobi hear it tomorrow?”

“I don't know.”

She disliked the answer less than she had in the first days. It left room for them to plan a second way to carry the warning when a window returned.

The baffle clicked once. Mariya took the tile with the forty-five names to Nerissa and Orran. She showed them where the newest marks were and which people needed help to stand. If another pulse took her balance or her breath, someone else could make the count. Nerissa corrected one mark. Orran asked whether the wider service recess would fit a stretcher. Mariya did not know. He offered to test it when the fields quieted.

They were still trapped. They had lost Thesia, Pehl, and Annet. Mariya could no longer promise that every person would leave. She could tell the truth to the people who remained, and let them help decide what the truth required next.
