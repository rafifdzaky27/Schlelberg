# SCHLELBERG

## Chapter Seven — Names on Clay

On the sixth morning after the seed levy, Mariya broke one of the king's seals.

She did it with a narrow piece of wood, lifting the clay from the cord so that the bull's head remained almost whole. It was a small deception, and she disliked how readily her hands found it. Behind her, the granary was empty. The first light had not reached the jars.

The lower quarter had no barley left for the day's bread. A woman from the shore had brought three fish, but they would not feed the children whose fathers had been kept at the old storehouse. An older household had spent its last pulses and was living on figs too hard to chew. Mariya could wait for the next coastal delivery; she could not make a hungry child wait with her.

The granary wall held the clay marks of families who had deposited seed before the occupation. Some had brought a full basket; some had poured a handful from a cloth. The jar she approached belonged to all of them in uneven shares. She knew which household had carried grain uphill despite a sick child, which had argued about keeping more for the next sowing, and which had given because the village would otherwise have no field to plant. The king's seal had been pressed over those smaller choices as though his mark made the contents his.

She took a shallow bowl from the jar she had opened. Each scoop felt heavier than it was. The grain smelled sound. A measure of it could keep six people through the day if it was ground with what the mill still held. In the field, that same measure might become enough seed for a row that would feed them next year.

She replaced the cord and pressed the old seal back onto it. The edge would betray her to anyone who looked closely. On the back of her waxed board, where she kept the household portions, she cut a private mark for the withdrawal. She did not put a name beside it.

At the mill, she gave the grain to Thesia, who was waiting with her sister's two children. Hessah's mother helped turn the stone. No one asked where the barley had come from. Mariya understood that their silence might be gratitude, or fear of being asked to testify. She let them work.

The handstone rasped over the grain. The younger child wanted to turn it and was allowed three slow circles before his arms tired. Thesia showed him how to gather the flour from the edge without spilling it into the dust. Hessah's mother worked the larger stone with her whole weight. She said the flour would be coarse, then took it to the oven anyway. Mariya had expected the stolen measure to become bread almost at once. Between the jar and the meal were hands, heat, water, and time she had not provided.

By midday the bread had gone. One child saved a piece in her sleeve for her father, who was among the workers below. Mariya did not tell her when he would come home. She had made enough promises on behalf of men with spears.

Nerissa found her washing the empty bowl.

“Which jar?”

Mariya kept the water running over the wood. “The one nearest the wall.”

“Did the households agree?”

“They were hungry.”

“I heard your answer. It isn't the one I asked for.”

Mariya set the bowl down. Nerissa's sleeves were rolled to the elbow; a smear of medicine darkened one cuff. She had come from Samon's house, where the leg would still need tending after the visitor's brace stopped being new.

“I marked what I took,” Mariya said.

“For whom?”

“For when we meet.”

“And if somebody opens the jar before then?”

“Then they'll see it.”

“A broken seal tells them who used the grain. It doesn't tell them why you thought you alone could decide.”

Mariya wanted to answer that she had been the one awake before dawn, that the children would not have thanked a meeting for making their food arrive tomorrow. Both things were true. Neither made the other households' share hers to spend without a word.

“I'll tell them,” she said.

Nerissa dried her hands on her skirt. “Tell them before someone else finds the mark.”

Mariya nodded. When the midwife left, she turned the board face down. She had meant what she said; she also knew she would delay until she had an amount precise enough to defend. The habit felt too much like the seal she had pressed back into place.

***

The missing labor names led her to the well.

Every day the palace men demanded water for the crews under the ancestor terrace. The delivery went by the lower lane and returned with fewer vessels than it took. Mariya waited where the mill wall shaded the path and counted the bearers. Four village adults went down. Three came back at dusk. The fourth was reassigned to the night shift without being entered on the public tablet.

At the well, the scribe said the worker had been counted with the quarry crew.

“Then let me see the quarry tablet,” Mariya said.

“It is at the ridge.”

“I can walk there.”

“No passage without a permit.”

“Then you can send for it.”

The scribe lowered his stylus. He was a tired man whose hands had the calluses of someone who wrote more than he dug. He did not threaten her. He called a guard to move her away from the well.

Mariya went. The exchange had given her what she needed: the tablet was not here, and he expected her to stop at that answer. She waited until the guard was back by the shade, then circled through the potters' lane to the house of the reassigned worker. His wife knew nothing of a quarry posting. She had prepared supper for him and now sat with it growing cold.

There were two bowls on the low table, both filled. The wife had left the larger one beside the door, where her husband would find it after a late shift. She asked Mariya whether the scribe had written an injury. He had not. Then she asked whether Mariya had seen anyone carried out under cloth. Mariya had not seen that either. She gave the answers separately. A single “I don't know” would make the woman imagine both possibilities at once and might sound as though Mariya had not bothered to ask.

The wife told Mariya the man always tied his work sandal twice because the heel had split. It was a detail no tablet kept. Mariya put it on her own board beside the missing name. If she saw a sandal through the ration hatch, she could ask before the guard moved its wearer away.

“Don't go to the captain yet,” Mariya said. “Let me ask the water carriers.”

The woman looked at the bowl on the table. “If he's below, why did they say he's uphill?”

“I don't know.”

It was an answer Mariya was learning to give more often, and still hated.

Lios found her at the lower cistern. He carried a cup made from the bottom of his broken jug. The rim had been ground smooth. He offered it for her water without explaining why he had brought it; she drank and returned it carefully.

“They use the passage behind the old ovens,” he said. “Not the stairs by the well.”

“Who?”

“The ones who carry water down after dark. I went with them before the rope came off.”

“Where does it meet the storehouse?”

“At a door with a broken red mark. They leave full jars there. Somebody opens it from inside.”

He put his finger on the cistern's stone rim and drew the turn of the path. It was a child's route, remembered because he had walked it carrying too much weight. Mariya knew the ovens. If Lios's door was where he placed it, the public labor stair did not account for everyone going below.

“You don't need to go again,” she said.

“I wasn't offering.”

“I know.”

He took back the cup. “My aunt says I should stop looking down the old lane. She says if I keep thinking about it they'll put the rope back.”

“Your aunt wants you safe.”

“I want that too.” He hesitated. “Does wanting it make me a coward?”

Mariya thought of Tobious pulling him up the stairs while a man died behind them. “No. It means you know what happened.”

He looked at the cup in his hands. “It holds less now.”

“It's still yours.”

He went to fill it again, though he had not emptied it. Mariya waited until he was out of sight before tracing his route in her own board. She had a place to watch and a name to ask about. The palace tablet had offered neither.

***

At the shore, the fishers were arguing with a group of grain carriers from the next inlet. Therren's men had raised the passage toll, and the carriers wanted payment in oil as well as a share of anything they brought. Mariya knew one of them, a woman who had borrowed Aras's millstones in the spring after her own cracked.

Their boats were pulled above the tide on rollers slick with fish oil. A boy was scraping barnacles from a hull while the adults argued; he kept listening because the toll would decide whether he went to sea with them tomorrow or walked the ridge with a basket. One carrier had already paid the soldiers and wanted the others to pay too, before refusal singled out his household. The woman Mariya knew said a second toll would leave too little oil for the lamps used on the night crossing. Nobody at the shore was choosing between courage and cowardice. They were choosing which risk their families would see first.

“If we keep the grain on your boats,” Mariya said, “the soldiers will take it when you beach.”

“If we carry it over the hill, they will take it on the road.”

“Then send what you can in small loads with the fig traders. They already have permission to pass.”

“Small loads feed fewer people.”

“They arrive.”

The carrier woman looked toward the occupied ridge. “You want us to break the toll?”

“I want you to tell the other inlets what is happening here. If their carts stop for one day, the captain has a problem too.”

“And if he sends soldiers?”

“Then we'll have to decide together whether a day is worth it.”

“My mother lives beyond the ridge,” the woman said. “If the carts stop, her bread stops with them.”

“Then we do not call a stoppage without arranging hers,” Mariya answered. “Tell me which households cannot wait a day.”

The woman named three. Mariya wrote them before she wrote any proposed action. She was learning that a demand could only be as strong as the people it could keep fed while they made it.

Mariya had no authority to pledge other villages to danger. She could ask them to see the leverage they shared. The carrier promised to speak with two more households before sending an answer. It was less than Mariya wanted. It was a real promise she could count.

That evening, Panjios's supply porter came through the lower lane. He had no grain this time, only medical cloth and a small bundle of service parts. He would return by Salt Gate before the next scheduled opening. Mariya wrapped a scrap of linen around one part and scratched a leaf through the wax with her thumbnail. Beneath it she made seven short strokes: the original labor group as she knew it. On another fold she marked four, the number the palace tablet admitted. She added the name of the man reassigned at the well and the route Lios had shown her.

“This goes to Tobious,” she told the porter.

“There are limits on what I can carry.”

“It is one strip of cloth.”

“I meant what can be said about the site.”

“If you leave it here, you will have to tell him yourself why you did.”

The porter put it under the service parts. She watched him close the bundle. She could not know whether the message would survive the station clerk, the route, or the people who liked their records short. She could make it legible to the one person who would recognize the leaf.

When he left, she climbed to the granary. The sealed jar waited in the dark, a hairline fracture visible in the clay she had pressed back around the cord. She had counted seven workers and four names. She had measured out grain and hidden the measure. The difference between her and the scribe did not absolve her. It told her how urgently she needed to put her own account where other people could challenge it.
