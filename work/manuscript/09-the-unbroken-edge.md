# SCHLELBERG

## Chapter Nine — The Unbroken Edge

The blade was shorter than the one Tobious had imagined when he was on his knees in the granary yard.

It lay under a clear cover in the memorial gallery, beside a shallow basin of black stone. The metal was pale and almost dull. A narrow dark channel ran from the guard toward the tip, stopping before the edge. The grip had been bound and rebound; one section wore a darker repair, another the faded impression of a hand that had held it for years. A small opaque core sat at the pommel. Nothing in the case moved. No light passed over the blade to tell him it was waiting.

Visitors had left tokens around the basin: a bent service pin, a child's painted stone, a strip of cloth worked with the old Warden crest. Someone had scratched a name on the pin so small Tobious could not read it. At home a village might hang a bronze spear above a hearth and tell the story of the arm that bore it. Veyr had kept a sword beneath a case, surrounded by offerings from people whose lives it had touched. He wanted to believe the object deserved them. He was wary of what people had been asked to forget in order to put it there.

“Severance,” Panjios said. “The older title is the Unbroken Edge.”

Tobious rested his hands on the rail. “The edge has been repaired.”

“Several times. The name tells you what people needed it to mean.”

Panjios opened a side panel showing the repairs. One had been made after a bridge collapse, another when a bearer held a seam through a cooling failure. The dark wrapping at the grip was newer than either. No bearer had carried the exact blade as it had first been forged. What endured was the work of people who refused to discard it when it could still protect a passage.

“Then why call it unbroken?” Tobious asked.

“Because the story was written before anyone saw the repairs up close,” Panjios said. He closed the panel gently. “The story kept people coming to the case. The repairs kept the blade worth coming for.”

Beyond the case, a wall carried the names of Wardens who had died defending crossings. Each name held its own small light. At the center was an image of an earlier bearer standing on a bridge whose far end had filled with Iwang. In the image she held Severance against a seam while people ran behind her. It was not a picture made with a painter's brush. The figures moved through a brief repeated interval: the bearer turning her head, a child lifted over the rail, the bridge's darkening span.

Tobious watched it twice.

“Did she live?”

“Long enough to close the route,” Panjios said. “Not much longer.”

The inscription described an evacuation in a sentence. It gave one number for the people saved and one name for the person who held the blade. Tobious felt the old desire stir in him: to be the one who could stand at a breaking point and change what followed. He also thought of the child in the image. Someone had lifted her. The blade-bearer had both hands occupied.

Ypin stopped beside him, a tool roll tucked under his arm.

“The maintenance crews aren't on the wall,” he said.

Panjios heard. “No.”

“Four teams held the bridge services open. Their report is in the archive. The inscription says she brought the people out.”

“She kept the route alive,” Panjios said.

“So did they.”

Tobious looked back at the moving image. He did not want it less because it was incomplete. He wanted to know who had decided that one visible courage was easier to remember than many.

“Could they put the names here?” he asked.

“They could,” Ypin said. “If somebody with the authority cared enough to request the correction.”

Panjios turned toward him. For a moment Tobious thought they would argue. Instead the older Warden said, “Bring me the report. We'll make the request.”

Ypin's expression did not change. Tobious had come to know that he was more skeptical when given what he wanted than when refused it.

“The request has been made before,” Ypin said.

Panjios looked at the wall. “Then bring the earlier answer too.”

They left the gallery by a door that passed beneath the moving bridge. Tobious glanced up at the woman with Severance. In the repeated moment she turned her head, always toward something outside the frame. He could not see what she had seen before she made the cut.

***

The calibration room was smaller and less beautiful. Cables lay in marked channels on the floor. Three workers checked their instruments before allowing anyone near the blade. Edda had already certified Tobious's wrist for limited contact. She remained by the medical station, close enough to stop the test if his pulse or the root load changed too quickly.

One worker took a dry cloth to the grip and cleaned away the marks visitors had left on the case. Another checked the core without touching its surface. The third set a blank injury slate beside Edda's station. They had done this for other bearers and would record the result if this bearer failed. Tobious felt suddenly less like a hero arriving at a relic and more like a patient waiting for a procedure whose risks everyone else knew by name.

Another trainee lifted Severance first. She could hold it, turn it, and use its ordinary edge against a practice target. The dark channel remained dark. The core offered no sign that she was anything but a person carrying a well-kept weapon.

She handed it to Panjios. He set it on the stand before Tobious.

“No oath?” Tobious asked.

“There are oaths for Wardens. This isn't one of them.”

“No test of character?”

“If it had one, our history would be simpler.”

Panjios said it quietly, then seemed to regret how much the answer held. Tobious could not ask for its meaning now. Three technicians were waiting, and the blade lay before him.

He placed his left hand on the repaired grip. It was warm from Panjios's hold. With the right, he touched the flat near the guard. Pressure gathered behind his teeth, the same pressure he had felt at the broken Receiver. The opaque core clouded, then cleared from within. A fine line traveled along the dark channel.

The instruments changed tone. Ypin named the readings to the senior calibrator, who confirmed each one. Tobious had expected a surge through his arm, perhaps a voice. He felt only the weight of the blade becoming more exact. It had been made for a purpose; the part of him the Receiver had taken could complete its circuit.

The line in the channel reached the old repair and hesitated. Tobious thought it would stop there. The calibrator held up a hand, asking the room to wait. The line crossed the repaired section and continued to the tip. No one cheered. Ypin let out the breath he had been holding. Tobious understood the crossing as a test of the work done on the blade, not of the man holding it. A weaker repair would have broken the circuit no matter how badly he wanted to be chosen.

“The reference accepts you,” the calibrator said.

“The machine accepted me by accident,” Tobious said.

She looked at him over the instrument. “Yes.”

The answer did not diminish the blade in his hand. It diminished the ease with which someone else might claim the blade proved him worthy.

Ypin showed him the service record. He pointed to an empty slot marked for a synthetic regulator and then to the reading from Tobious's contact. The regulator had broken before the Storehouse opening. In its absence, the system had used the first viable living signal against the exposed frame. Tobious remembered the blood on his knuckles. He had thought it might have called the door to him. It had merely helped carry his contact into a broken part.

“Could you put a new regulator in?” he asked.

“We would if we had an equivalent reference material,” Ypin said. “We can replace the casing. The core is another matter.”

“Then use another person. Someone who knows what they're doing.”

Panjios answered before Ypin could. “We can transfer a living reference under controlled conditions. It takes seven uninterrupted days with every receiver idle and both people present. We would install a substitute regulator, hold the root steady, and calibrate the change. It is possible.”

“Why haven't you?”

“The damaged Storehouse isn't reliably idle. The other two routes still carry essential service. You can be trained now. We haven't yet been given seven quiet days.”

Ypin added, “A willing expert would still have to become the new reference. Expertise doesn't make the seven days shorter.”

Tobious looked at the sword. He could set it down. The chamber would not punish him for doing so. Aras would still be beside a broken gate. Panjios would still need to find a way to close it. The possibility of another bearer mattered because it made the choice real, even if the easiest available person was him.

“Then show me what I would be doing,” he said.

The calibrator fitted a line from the stand to a practice barrier in the next room. She explained the modes without ceremony: an ordinary edge, a guarded local cut, and a terminal function intended for the root itself. The last required prolonged living contact and consumed the active core. Panjios did not dwell on it. The current task was to keep a receiver stable, not to destroy every route.

Tobious heard enough to remember. A tool that could end a connection might one day matter differently from a tool that kept it open.

***

On the practice floor, the barrier rose like a slab of dusk. It was not an Iwang and not a doorway. It held a measured field that would register whether Severance could separate one marked band without damaging the space beside it.

Jorra stood at Tobious's right. Sennos covered the exit. Ypin monitored the barrier and held a reserve shield stand ready. Suss watched from the far edge of the room; Edda watched Tobious's hands.

“Three heartbeats to find the contact,” Suss said. “Do not chase the light. Keep the edge where the field presses back.”

Tobious advanced. The blade seemed lighter once he moved, but his wrist objected when he tried to control it with both hands. He kept the right near the guard and let the left carry the weight.

He set the edge to the marked band. A line gathered in Severance's channel. One heartbeat. The barrier pushed sideways. He followed it too eagerly. His own shield faded, and he felt cool air against the back of his neck. Two heartbeats. The edge lost contact. The line died.

“Withdraw,” Jorra said.

He did. She lifted cover between him and the barrier while the sword settled. Edda checked his pulse; it had climbed, but the root readings were within the range the calibrator wanted.

“I had it,” Tobious said.

“For one heartbeat,” Ypin said. “It needed three.”

“I know how many.”

“Then knowing isn't the part you need help with.”

Suss came forward. He pointed to Tobious's feet. “You followed the band with your shoulders. Move one foot. Let the arm remain quiet.”

Tobious tried the motion without the blade. It felt small. The first attempt had felt brave, as though forcing the sword through resistance might prove something. The small motion kept his balance.

They waited for the core to settle. A clock above Ypin's board counted thirty seconds from the end of the failed contact. Tobious watched each one. In the granary a soldier had ended his defense in less time than this sword needed to become ready again. A weapon that made a man wait was difficult to imagine as a miracle.

On the second attempt, Jorra told him she would cover his side when his field fell. He made himself believe she meant it. He placed his left foot, set the edge, and kept the right hand steady without asking it to bear the strain. One heartbeat. The barrier pressed back. Two. He felt the temptation to turn his shoulders and stayed still. Three.

The marked band opened. Severance passed through a space no thicker than a stalk. The rest of the barrier held. Its dark surface trembled once, then quieted.

Tobious stepped back into Jorra's cover. His shield returned after the blade withdrew. A low sound went around the room. It was the crew acknowledging a successful calibration, not a crowd naming a king. He found himself wishing Mariya had seen it. Then he was glad she had not been made to stand near an untested root just to witness him use a sword.

“Again,” he said.

Ypin held up a finger. The recovery clock had begun anew. “When it settles.”

Tobious waited. Suss studied the clean cut and gave him a brief nod.

“Useful,” the commander said.

Tobious had been called useful by Therren when the captain wanted his labor, by Darran when he wanted a hand on a broken frame. In Suss's voice it sounded like a place he might choose for himself. He knew the difference should matter. For one breath, the pleasure of being needed made it hard to remember why.
