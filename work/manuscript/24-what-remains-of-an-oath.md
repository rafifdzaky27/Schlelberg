# SCHLELBERG

## Chapter Twenty-Four — What Remains of an Oath

Mariya's packet arrived with forty-five living names.

Tobious read the three deaths after the names of the living, as she had arranged them. Thesia had died in the first test, Pehl during the surge, Annet after it. He did not look at Ypin when the Day-56 warning played. Its double click and field pressure matched the Storehouse signature he had kept on his private device. He had spent days calling that trace incomplete. Mariya had been recording what it did to bodies while he waited for a second test.

He listened again to the count, this time without the recording. Ypin set the wafer down and read each name aloud. Tobious stopped him at Hessah, whose condition required a stretcher, and at Orran, who had offered to hold a failing brace. They were no longer a number at the bottom of a rescue calculation. He wrote their tasks in the margin of the route sketch. Beside three other names there were no tasks to write. He had seen the warning that might have spared them, and he had hidden it because reporting it would have ended his place beside the door.

“If I take this to Edda,” Ypin said, “she has to know when you first saw it.”

“She will.”

“I am not asking for a brave answer. I am asking because I put my hand on that coupling after you told me the trace was unresolved.”

Tobious remembered how readily Ypin had trusted him with the bench. He had been grateful for that trust while keeping the one piece of evidence most likely to change Ypin's judgment. “I should have told you before you touched it,” he said.

Ypin did not forgive him. He copied the dated trace and sealed it beside his own calibration log, where neither of them could alter the sequence without leaving a mark. Then they went to the clinic.

At Edda's clinic meeting room, he placed the device beside the authenticated archive copies.

“I saw the spike after the cooling shutter rescue,” he said. “I didn't report it. I signed the next operation anyway.”

Edda asked him to give the exact date and who had seen the line. Ypin confirmed he had asked for the full route and Tobious had delayed filing it. The two cooling-station workers who had survived the shutter failure had already given statements about field heat and Suss's command decisions. A third worker from the Day-57 escape described Panjios holding the isolation door and Suss striking him. Edda collected each account separately. She did not ask them to agree on details they could not all have seen.

One of the cooling-station workers had lost two fingers when the shutter dropped. He used his good hand to turn the route display toward Tobious. “You can say the pressure was here. I can say I felt it before the field rose. Neither of us saw who moved that gate.”

“Suss gave the command,” Tobious said.

“Did you hear him?” Edda asked.

“No. I saw the authorization on the console afterward.”

She separated those two facts in her notes. Tobious had wanted the injured man to strengthen his case. Instead the man was making the case strong enough to survive someone trying to break it. Panjios's death did not become more true when Tobious claimed knowledge he did not have.

Edda replayed a portion of the testimony from the Day-57 worker. The worker knew the sound of Suss's strike, because he had been at the isolation door. He did not know whether Panjios was still conscious after it. Tobious closed his eyes. There were hours of that day he had wanted to fill with certainty, as if certainty could give Panjios a better ending. Edda let the recording finish without asking him to make one.

“This supports a casualty review and an order to preserve service access,” she said. “It does not give me a power to convict a field commander in this room.”

“If we wait for a tribunal, the root fails on Day Eighty-four,” Tobious said.

“Then give the review an emergency plan that can be acted on now.”

She issued a request to the civil marshals for independent access at the Root Hall, medical records held outside Concord command, and protection for essential service crews. Three marshals agreed to take the order. One operations supervisor refused to sign without a complete routing trace. Another would corroborate the local load data but would not confront Suss at his post. Jorra and Sennos agreed to support a rescue after reviewing the names and the route; they remained Wardens responsible for everyone at the sites, not soldiers belonging to Tobious.

The first marshal to arrive carried a narrow slate of emergency powers. He read it to Edda before he read her request. He could keep a corridor open for inspection; he could not disarm a commander without an active threat witnessed at the site. He could secure clinic records; he could not promise that the Root Hall would obey a finding issued after the root had already failed.

“Then come while there is still a root,” Edda said.

He signed for the corridor. The second and third signed for the records and the service crews. None signed for Tobious. When they left, Edda made three copies of their orders and sent one by a route Suss did not control. She had seen too many emergency permissions vanish into an office where the emergency had been caused.

Jorra studied the ancient stretcher sketch before accepting her part. “If the cut happens here, who covers the patient while the blade settles?”

“Sennos,” Tobious said.

“He cannot cover the patient and the person making the cut. We need a second stand or a different waiting place.”

“Then help us find one.”

“I will. But I am not putting my name on a plan that assumes my shield can be in two places.”

She wrote *conditional* beside her signature. It was the first honest word on the page.

The coalition did not become a crowd chanting for justice. It became a list of people who had agreed to particular work, and of people who had not.

***

On Ypin's bench, they built a model with three stones for the ancient accesses and a fourth for the root in Veyr. Tobious brought Mariya's sketch of the ninth step and the stretcher turn. The difference between the narrow passage and the wider recess was the width of a person's shoulders when lying down. It decided whether the weak could leave with everyone else.

Ypin laid the load traces beside Edda's independent injury record. Since the Day-57 breach, the root had oscillated in broad surges. Every trough between them was shorter than the one before. They tested the known baffle clicks against those troughs. A low-load interval would reach the Storehouse on Days Eighty-two and Eighty-three. Before then, cutting more than one tether would push the strain through the people still inside. After that interval, the next rise would become terminal on Day Eighty-four.

“This is Schlelberg in the inhabited volumes now,” Ypin said. “Not only a seam in one chamber. The pressure peaks under their floor and under our service ward. Each side has begun to feel the other's failing support.”

Edda drew a line from the future clinic injuries to Mariya's baffle times. It did not turn the two places into one place. It showed that a person could be harmed in either without knowing whose demand had reached them through the root.

Ypin made them watch the traces at the speed the machine had recorded them. At first Tobious saw only two lines swelling and falling. Then Ypin placed Mariya's account beneath the display: the double click, the pause in which voices disappeared, the moment Pehl fell. On the next rise the root line climbed a fraction before the Storehouse line did. The people under the village had felt the future's demand before anyone in Veyr saw an alarm.

Edda asked Ypin to run a single-release projection. A white mark left the ancient branch. For a breath the other marks stayed level. Then each remaining mark rose. The effect was small enough on the screen that Tobious could imagine someone calling it tolerable. Edda put the injury record beside it. Annet's pulse had failed during a smaller rise.

“That model doesn't tell us which person carries the extra strain,” she said. “It tells us we cannot choose a number and call the rest a rounding error.”

Ypin reset the projection. He was too careful with his hands for Tobious to miss how angry he was.

“Why can't we take five a day?” Tobious asked.

“We can take an individual if we place the rest of the load somewhere,” Ypin said. “Suss's offer to take Mariya was physically possible. The remaining people would pay for each early release. Five a day would keep raising that cost.”

He marked the already tested limited extraction. Its sink could buffer one person briefly; it was not a shelter for forty-five. Tobious had wanted to call it a lie because it was cruel. Seeing its real capacity made the cruelty clearer.

“If Mariya had come out when Suss offered,” Tobious said, “would the surge have killed someone?”

“I can't name the person or promise death,” Ypin said. “I can say the pressure would have risen in every remaining tether, and Suss knew he had not built a sink for them.”

There was a kind of mercy in a precise answer, even when it withheld absolution. Mariya had refused without possessing the model. She had trusted what the floor told her about the bodies beside her. Tobious had possessed a model and once used its uncertainty as a place to hide.

Edda moved the future-city layer over the root. “If we destroy this now, the captives' floor collapses and these service blocks lose cooling before they can be transferred to independent supply.”

Tobious touched the map near the old west drain. “The wider recess will flood if the Storehouse branch redirects water when the tethers release. We can divert the old channel first.”

“Can you do it from Veyr?” Ypin asked.

“No. Samon and the village crew know that stone better than my diagram.”

The plan acquired people as it grew. Ancient workers would brace the recess and clear the drain. Mariya's group would stage stretchers, signal baffle intervals, and name who could move. Jorra and Sennos would protect the corridor. Tobious would cut sequentially under cover, waiting for the core to settle instead of using the higher gain. Ypin would manage the future-side load and relay. Edda would prepare clinics, water reserves, and housing for the city if its root services went dark. None of those pieces could stand alone.

They found a shorter route on the model. It would have sent the return load through the occupied lower service ward in Veyr for one hour. Tobious knew the residents there: Ypin's host, the child who had written names on water containers, the families who had waited beside a broken reservoir. The shortcut would make the ancient release easier by treating those homes as a sink.

Edda called the ward's water steward on an open line. She asked which lines could be isolated and which could not. The steward went room by room. A clinic's cold store would lose its medicines first. Two upper floors could be moved if given a day's notice. In the lower rooms, people had brought their containers back after the reservoir repair and begun filling them because they had been told the danger was over. The steward did not know why Edda was asking.

“Are you shutting us down again?” he said.

“We are considering a route that would put heat into your block. I am checking what it would touch.”

“You can check from there. We would have to live inside the answer.”

Edda thanked him and ended the call. Tobious did not ask her to soften what the steward had said.

“No,” he said. “We don't do that.”

Ypin turned the model to show the longer path. It required one more holding interval and additional manual cover. Edda asked how many residents the shorter line would have exposed. Ypin gave the estimate. She wrote the rejected option into the meeting record so that no one could later pretend it had never been considered.

***

The terminal function had been listed in Severance's calibration record since Day Eighteen. Tobious knew it could destroy the root reference. He had not known what a complete evacuation would leave him time to do.

Ypin put seven tokens on the bench for the uninterrupted idle days needed to transfer the living reference. The captives would remain tethered until the low-load interval. The last stretcher could leave late on Day Eighty-three. The root would need to be cut by Day Eighty-four. There was no week between those events, and no fourth access hidden in a map. When the root's mineral reference was consumed, Storehouse, Salt, and Quarry would all go dark.

Tobious tried moving the first token to a day before the rescue. Ypin removed it. A living receiver would still be under Aras. Tobious tried transferring the reference during the two quiet hours they expected after each baffle cycle. Ypin slid all seven tokens back to the beginning; the idle count restarted whenever the field took a body into its load. They repeated the exercise until Tobious stopped searching for an arrangement of stones that would let him keep both sides of the door.

“Let me see the terminal record,” Edda said.

Ypin projected the old calibration. It recorded a complete cut only at the root seam. The operator had to keep contact while the reference consumed the core. The warning did not say *death*. It listed field burns, loss of hand function, and failure of the cut if contact broke early. Tobious read the failure line twice. Dying would not make the fifteen heartbeats count if his grip slipped at the ninth.

Jorra asked whether another person could brace him. Ypin said a second body at the seam would become another path for the load. A shield could cover the approach, but the final contact had to be his. Edda wrote that distinction into the medical plan. They could prepare for an injured survivor; they could not pretend his fear was irrelevant to the procedure.

“I have to be at the seam in Veyr,” Tobious said.

“Yes,” Ypin answered. “Fifteen continuous heartbeats. Your live contact. The core is spent with the root.”

He looked at the ancient side of the map. He could cross back to Aras for the rescue, but to finish it he would have to return to Veyr. After the cut he would still be here, if he lived. Mariya could cross before closure if she chose. There was no rule making her remain in Aras. That decision would belong to her, not to the sword or to an official count.

“Tell her every part,” he said. “The risk to the city, the last crossing, the fact that she can come here.”

“We'll send the complete proposal on the next diagnostic window,” Ypin said.

Tobious dictated his section of the message without looking at Ypin's script. He named the three accesses and the time by which they would fail. He named the seven idle days they did not have. When he reached Mariya's crossing, he began, *If you can bear leaving Aras—* and stopped. It turned her choice into a test of what she could endure for him. He started again.

“You can cross before the closure. There is room for you here. I want you here. If you choose to stay, I will still make the cut.”

He asked Ypin to keep the first recording only as a discarded draft. The corrected one belonged in the packet. His own desire could be plain without becoming another tether.

Edda studied him. “Are you agreeing to stay because you think guilt gives us your life?”

“I'm agreeing because I am the current reference and we don't have seven days to move it. I don't want to die. I want the people out first.”

She nodded, not as a blessing but as confirmation that he had heard the terms.

Tobious assigned the first tasks aloud. Jorra challenged the distance between the shield stand and the stretcher turn. He let her move it. Edda demanded a second medical team. Ypin asked who could stop a cut if the ancient count failed. Tobious said Mariya and Nerissa could, from inside, and Jorra from the cover line. He put their names beside that authority.

He had once thought leadership meant being the person no one could stop. The plan became safer when he gave several people the right to tell him to wait.

Edda copied the stop signals onto three separate slates. One would go with the future cover team, one to the ancient receiver, and one to the clinic. They did not know whether any single line would survive the final surge. Outside the meeting room the city continued its shift change. A food porter asked when the lower ward would have its cooling restored; a patient asked whether the new shelter would take her father. Edda answered both before returning to the map. The rescue had not yet begun, and already it belonged to people beyond the room.
