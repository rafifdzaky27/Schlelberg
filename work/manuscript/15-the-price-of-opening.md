# SCHLELBERG

## Chapter Fifteen — The Price of Opening

Suss placed two figures beside one another on the debrief wall: twelve and forty-eight.

The first belonged to people who had walked out through the safe stair. The second belonged to people still breathing inside the annex, now held by the load Tobious had moved. Tobious could see the route he had cut, its bright line ending at Severance's recorded contact. He wanted to say the guard had struck Annet. He wanted to say the Iwang had come through the wall. Both things were true. Neither changed where the light had gone when he used the blade.

The twelve appeared as pale marks climbing the near stair. A delay in the record made them move again and again, always reaching the same point of safety. The far-floor marks did not move at all after the branch collapsed. Tobious asked Ypin to stop the replay. Suss let it run to the end first. He wanted the pattern visible; Tobious wanted to look away from it. Neither reason changed the tracing.

“Put the warning back,” Tobious said when the display cleared.

Ypin did. The far-floor path had been marked before the cut, faint but legible. The symbol for unmeasured living load lay beside it. Tobious remembered seeing the mark and telling himself he would have time to fix whatever it meant after he freed the people nearest him. He had not forgotten a warning in the heat of battle. He had ranked it below his own chosen action because the people it described stood farther away.

“You ordered us to leave them there,” he said.

“After the cut,” Suss answered. “I ordered you not to make it.”

Panjios stood at the far side of the room. He looked as tired as Tobious felt. “I asked for the support map. We did not have it.”

Jorra was beside him, her shield arm in a sling for the morning. “I covered the cut,” she said. “I saw the warning too late to pull him clear without leaving the twelve at the stair. I need that in the record.”

Suss entered her statement. Tobious wanted to tell her she had not made the decision. She knew that; she had named what she could and could not see. He understood that her account would keep him from using the team's presence as a blur in which his choice vanished.

“Annet was down,” Tobious said. “The guard had a spear.”

“I saw her,” Panjios said. “I did not say you imagined the danger.”

Suss expanded the recording. A thin warning had flashed on Ypin's board before Tobious made the cut. It had marked the transfer to the far floor. Tobious remembered seeing it. At the time it had been a prediction beside an immediate blow. Now the far floor held forty-eight names.

“I thought the second line would take a few breaths,” he said.

“You did not know,” Suss replied. “That is why we map.”

“The system shouldn't have put people there.”

“Agreed,” Panjios said. “You still cut while they were there.”

Tobious looked at him. Panjios had spent weeks teaching him when to withdraw. His voice now held neither anger nor the comfort of absolution. Tobious could hate being measured by that standard; he could not claim it had been hidden from him.

Ypin arrived with a packet from the ancient diagnostic relay. He played Mariya's voice for them. The room went quiet at the first count: forty-seven alive, one dead. Mariya named Thesia. Tobious had seen her at the granary once holding bread for her sister's children; he had not known her well. He heard in Mariya's voice exactly how well she had.

The recording continued with water, rations, a broken ledge, and a landing that could not yet free anyone. It ended with the request to tell Thesia's sister that the children had eaten. Tobious listened a second time because the first had left him holding only the number. The second gave him the sentence Mariya had chosen to send across centuries.

The Salt porter would not reach Thesia's sister before evening. Tobious asked whether they could send her voice immediately through the relay. Ypin showed him the dead interval. The diagnostic pulse had already closed; the ancient side would not hear him until the next scheduled opening, and the sister lived above the field in any case. The quickest truthful route was a person carrying the message along a road. Tobious had crossed twenty-six centuries in an hour, yet he could not make a grieving household hear one sentence before a porter walked there.

“We tell her sister through the village network,” he said.

“Yes,” Ypin answered. “The Salt porter can take a notice.”

“And we get the other forty-seven.”

“With a plan,” Panjios said.

Tobious turned away from the wall. Twelve people were safe because he had acted. He would not pretend they should have remained in danger to make his account cleaner. Forty-eight had been stranded because he had acted without knowing enough. He would not use the twelve to erase them.

***

Edda examined the wrist he had used to steady Severance. The swelling had returned under the support. She cleaned a field burn at his shoulder and asked exactly when it had begun hurting. He told her about the cut. He did not ask for a treatment that would make it possible to repeat one immediately.

She gave him a cup of water and made him drink before describing the burn. When he failed to hold the cup steadily, she set it on the table rather than holding it to his mouth. The courtesy hurt. He was accustomed to having hands that could move a beam or lift a sack. He had built a new kind of usefulness around Severance and now wanted to prove it had not ended with one terrible decision. Edda's examination left no room for proof. His skin was damaged; his wrist needed rest; the people under Aras needed a plan that would not depend on him ignoring either fact.

Two of the cooling-station workers he had helped on Day 28 waited outside her room for routine follow-up. One recognized him and thanked him again. Tobious had expected the gratitude to feel good. Today it made him want to leave before she finished speaking. Edda noticed and asked them for their account of the phase pressure, not for an account of his heroism. They described the wall's narrowing and the heat after Suss dropped the gradient. Their observations were useful. Their survival was also true.

One worker had returned to a shift that required fine adjustments. She said she still sometimes felt the wall heat in a room whose power was off. Edda asked whether it happened at work, at home, or when she heard a service alarm. The worker said she did not know yet. Tobious waited for Edda to connect the account to the ancient floor. Edda recorded it as the woman's injury, not as an instrument for his argument. After the worker left, she showed Tobious the aggregate field temperature at the cooling station and the mark from his own shoulder. Those observations could be compared with Mariya's account without requiring the worker's fear to be treated as proof of something she had not witnessed.

Edda opened the current Storehouse report. “The public casualty notice names no ancient patients. That conflicts with what you saw and with the injuries your recorder measured.”

“Can you order them released?” Tobious asked.

“I can certify harm, ask for an emergency review, and protect my patients' records. I cannot command the root by signing a page. I need a safe extraction plan, a place for the people to go, and enough evidence that a civil order can reach the staff who will carry it out.”

“You have Mariya's voice.”

“I have a voice and your field injuries. I want the names of the twelve who came out. They are on the ancient side, so someone has to bring their statements through a service route. I won't pretend they're in my waiting room.”

Tobious was grateful for the correction. He had been ready to imagine an inquiry that would solve the problem by existing. Edda's work began with beds, water, authority, and people able to stand between an order and a commander who disliked it.

“If I brought you the plan,” he asked, “would you act?”

“I would examine it, tell you where it fails, and act on what survives.”

It was the answer of someone who meant to help more than to be thanked.

He left the clinic with a clean dressing and no permission to wield the sword that day. Across the hall a service crew was replacing a failed water regulator. The old one had been set on a tray, split at a seam so small he would have missed it before he met Ypin. He watched the workers test the replacement twice before opening the line. The ward lost water for a little longer because they waited. Nobody thanked them for taking that time. The taps ran when they finished.

***

Suss asked Tobious to meet him without the larger team. Ypin came as the technician who could explain the field routing; Panjios was not invited. Tobious considered refusing. Mariya's packet had made refusal feel like something that would cost days he could not count for her.

On the table Suss placed a smaller plan than the one used for inspection. It isolated one person from the far floor, drew strain through a temporary sink, and returned that person by a watched Salt opening. The mechanism existed. The sink's capacity was limited; Suss showed the limit. He did not call it a general rescue.

Suss had also brought the roster of workers saved from the high platform when Tobious first came to Veyr. He laid it beside the ancient count. “I held that lift for one crew,” he said. “The rest of the platform had to stay closed while we did. If I had tried to bring everyone through the same opening at once, the lift would have fallen. You saw me choose.”

Tobious had seen it. He remembered Suss carrying an injured mechanic out while another team waited under cover. The comparison tempted him because it used a true rescue. It failed where the ledger changed people into an unnamed remainder with no visible turn to follow. He did not yet have a route for the forty-seven. Suss had a route for one.

“Mariya could come first,” he said. “You could speak with her before the next cycle.”

Tobious saw her on the wall of names, one among forty-seven. The thought of her walking out alive struck him with such force that he had to steady his hand on the table.

“Who takes her place?”

“The load spreads among the others. Each carries a measurable additional strain. We can keep it below the harm threshold for a single extraction.”

Suss slid the projection toward him. The line for Mariya went dark on the ancient side and brightened at Salt. It showed a person arriving, not merely a number removed. Tobious could almost feel her hand in his. The other lines stayed pale. He knew enough now to ask why none of them had names, and wanted badly to postpone the question until after she was safe.

Ypin moved toward the display. “Below which threshold?”

“The monitored one.”

“Those monitors call them unregistered biological load. They did not record Thesia's death as a protected casualty.”

Suss faced him. “I am showing you a real route. If you have a safer one with capacity today, put it on the table.”

Ypin did not. Tobious could hear the unfairness and the truth in Suss's challenge at once. The plan could save Mariya. It would ask the others to bear some cost they had not been asked about, measured by a system that had refused their names.

“She won't leave them,” Tobious said.

“Has she told you that since captivity?”

“I know her.”

“Then give her the choice. I can arrange the exception if you support continued anchor stabilization while we plan the rest.”

“For how long?” Tobious asked.

“Until we have a release sequence that holds.”

“Who decides it holds?”

Suss did not pretend the answer was a village council. “My operators, with the load data we can verify. You can remain in the room.”

“The people carrying the strain cannot.”

“They are in the room that matters to the machine. If you want them out, help me keep that machine intact.”

Tobious thought of the moment at the granary when Therren had counted seed while Mariya counted households. It was possible to care about survival and still choose a measure that made some lives disappear. Suss was offering him a way to bring Mariya to safety. He was also asking him to help preserve the very arrangement that kept the others below.

Tobious had once promised Mariya he would tell her what a crossing cost before deciding for her. Suss's offer gave him the possibility of honoring the first half while making the second half almost impossible: to see a door open for her and accept a refusal if she made it.

He did not agree. He asked Suss to show how the high-gain setting would hold the sink during release, how its heat would be measured, and who would sign the load distributed among the forty-six left behind. Suss answered the first two with diagrams. The third he said would be included in the operation order.

When they left, Ypin followed Tobious into the corridor.

“He named one person he can save,” Ypin said. “He made the others an amount of heat.”

“I heard him.”

“Did you?”

Tobious stopped. He wanted to tell Ypin that a man whose closest person was trapped had no need to be lectured about names. He wanted to tell him that if Mariya died while they sought a perfect list, the list would not hold her. Neither answer made Suss's count complete.

Ypin looked back toward the closed room. “Whose names are absent from his cost column?”

Tobious could not answer. That was why the question stayed with him.
