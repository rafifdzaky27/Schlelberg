# SCHLELBERG

## Chapter Fourteen — Inside the Hollow

Mariya counted forty-eight people before she counted the ways out.

The number took her three attempts. People moved when she approached, some because they feared another field would rise beneath their feet, some because the guard at the ration hatch ordered them back to the painted marks. Twice she counted the same man. Once she nearly missed Lios, who had tucked himself into the recess beneath a collapsed shelf, his cup between his knees.

“Say your name when I reach you,” she told them. “If you can't, someone who knows it can.”

Nerissa stood by a dry patch of floor and repeated each name onto the back of a broken clay tile. Mariya kept the order on her board. Annet's shoulder was swollen from the guard's blow. Hessah had a cut across her palm from catching a slipping water skin. Pehl could walk but trembled whenever the wall clicked. There were bruises and burns that had no obvious cause, where the field had tightened around people who tried to move away.

They were still on the ancient side of the Receiver. Mariya could see the stair they had entered by through a line of pale pressure. Beyond it lay Aras, close enough that a person could hear a cart in the lane above. Between her and the stair, the floor held them as surely as a locked door. When Pehl stepped across one painted mark, light rose around Annet's ankle. He stepped back, and it faded. The system treated their bodies as weights on a scale. It had no mark for the choice that made a person move.

Orran, a stoneworker with dust ground into the cracks of his hands, said they should send the strongest six through the service recess while the line was dim. “If some of us get out, we can bring help.”

“Some of us?” Annet asked. “Which six?”

“The ones who can pull a gate.”

Pehl looked toward the ration hatch. “My daughter is outside. I'm going if there is a path.”

“And my mother is outside,” Annet said. “That doesn't make your legs worth more than mine.”

Mariya had no answer that would satisfy them all. She had promised Tobious she would not let a blade decide what she could bear. She was now in a room where every decision seemed to press on someone else's ankles.

“We test the recess with a rope first,” she said. “No one crosses until we know what the baffle does.”

Orran frowned. “While we wait, those things gather.”

A pale strut touched the far wall, then withdrew when the field in its seam dimmed. Mariya could hear the mineral clicks from inside the stone. He was right that waiting had a cost. He was wrong that moving without a test made the cost smaller.

Aboveground people had begun calling the Iwang the Hollow Ones. The name made sense when one crossed a live seam: its open body showed the stone behind it, as though the wall had learned to walk. It did not tell Mariya how to move forty-eight people past it. She watched the seam dim and set the rope test for the next quiet interval.

Nerissa caught Mariya by the sleeve. “Assign the water. I can take the injured.”

“I know who needs it.”

“Then tell me. Don't make knowing into a reason to do it all.”

Mariya gave her the list of injuries. Pehl took the first water jars from the ration hatch. Thesia, whose feet were blistered from a torn sandal, offered to mark the sleeping places. Lios had seen the old red-marked service door; he described it but was not asked to enter it again. Orran and two others cleared a sanitation corner behind a low wall. It smelled bad almost at once. No one had planned the annex as a home for forty-eight bodies. Keeping it live for the root did not make it fit for human living.

Thesia marked sleeping places by household, then erased half the marks when Nerissa pointed out that the people nearest the active wall would need a clear route to the water. She did not defend the first arrangement. She moved the injured inward and asked two workers to sleep near the hatch because they could bear the guard's light. When Lios chose a place behind the shelf, she gave him a piece of cloth to hang over the sharp edge without asking him to leave the only corner that felt protected. He showed her the repaired cup he kept there. She said the repair was ugly and useful, which was how her sister described every household tool.

At the sanitation wall Orran found an old drain too narrow to carry waste by itself. He asked Pehl to bring loose tiles, and the two men made a shallow runnel from broken pieces. They disagreed about its slope until water proved Pehl right. Orran took the correction with a grunt. Later he returned with a second tile to keep the runnel from crossing a sleeping place. Mariya noticed how quickly forty-eight people became a set of local problems whose solutions required people to know one another. The root's count had no room for those skills.

Darran's guards brought grain and a thin broth that evening. One guard counted portions by marks on a tablet. Mariya placed herself at the hatch with her own board. Forty-eight went in; forty-eight portions had to come through. The guard grew impatient, but Darran told him to finish. They needed the people alive. Mariya did not yet know why. She meant to make that need serve the people for as long as she could.

The portions arrived in bowls whose rims had been chipped by repeated washing. Pehl showed Lios how to hold one without putting a thumb in the hot broth. Annet traded a piece of her bread for Hessah's place near the wall, where a draft helped her swollen shoulder. Thesia brought Mariya a bowl after the count. “Eat before the guard decides we have too many,” she said.

“I have already counted.”

“That has never stopped a man with a tablet.”

They ate sitting on the dry floor. Thesia described how her sister's younger child always stole the browned crust from the bottom of the cooking pot, then blamed a cat the family did not own. For a little while Lios tried to think of a name for the imaginary cat. When the wall clicked, conversation stopped, then resumed with a different rhythm. Thesia had given them something to speak about that the machine could not measure.

***

The ninth stair clicked through the night. Mariya could not see it from the sleeping recess, but the sound traveled along the old stone: one high note, two low. Every cycle made the water in their basin tremble. The pattern did not stop for people trying to sleep.

At first light, Thesia joined Mariya beside the service recess. The torn sandal had been tied with linen from Mariya's bundle. Thesia carried the clay tile on which Nerissa had written the names.

“My sister will come to the outer hatch,” she said. “She won't know I'm still here until someone tells her.”

“The twelve who escaped will tell the village.”

“Tell her yourself when the wafer opens.”

Mariya turned the wafer over. It held one brief recording from the night: forty-eight alive, ration status, no safe exit yet. The diagnostic window had not opened. She had not told anyone outside even that much.

“I will,” she said.

Thesia gave her the tile. “Make her hear the two children are eating. She'll think I took their share with me.”

Mariya began to answer, but Orran called from the recess. He had found a stone lip that might hold a rope. The group moved to watch. Mariya kept Thesia's request in mind as she checked the knot.

The test was simple enough to describe. They would send a weighted length of rope into the recess during the quiet interval. If it came back dry and unburned, one adult could examine the landing without crossing the tether line. Nobody would try to escape yet. Nerissa repeated the limit to the people gathering behind them. Orran repeated it too, reluctantly.

Thesia asked whether the rope could be fastened around a pillar before they sent it across. Orran said the pillar was cracked and might not hold if someone pulled hard. She suggested tying it around two stones instead. The test still would not carry a person; the knot would only keep the coil from whipping back across the floor. They did it her way.

Mariya tied the rope to a fragment of old masonry and pushed it across. The first baffle clicked. The stone slid onto the ledge and held. At the third click, the pale seam changed direction. The rope quivered but did not burn. Orran pulled it back. The weight returned with dust on one face and a faint blue mark on the other.

“There's a landing,” he said.

“And a field below it,” Mariya answered. “We don't know when it takes weight.”

They tried again, this time with less rope so the stone would strike the inner edge. Thesia held the spare coil behind Mariya. A guard at the ration hatch shouted for the workers to return to their marks. His voice made two people turn. The coil shifted. Mariya caught the rope, but the weighted stone had already begun to swing beyond the lip.

Thesia stepped toward her to steady the line. The ledge beneath Thesia's sandal gave way.

It was ordinary stone, fractured by years of damp and new field pressure. It broke before the next baffle interval. Thesia fell to her knees against the active boundary. Mariya seized her arm. Orran caught Mariya's belt. For one moment they held the living weight between them. Then the field tightened across the recess. Thesia cried out; her grip lost strength. Mariya tried to pull again and felt the tether around Orran's feet rise in answer.

“Let go,” Thesia said.

Mariya did not. Nerissa reached them and put both hands on Mariya's wrist, not to force her away but to keep her from following. Thesia's head struck the lower stone. Her body lay still beyond the bright seam, visible for one terrible breath before the field closed across it. There was no way to draw her back onto the ledge, only the broken stone and the rope trailing into the dark.

Orran remained holding Mariya's belt after the danger passed. His hands were shaking. He had argued that the strong should try first; he had seen strength fail against a floor that would not bear it.

Pehl tried the rope once more without stepping over the line, hoping its weight might reach Thesia. The tether rose around Annet's ankle when he pulled. He stopped before someone else fell. Nerissa tested the edge with a pole and found no purchase beneath the broken lip. They did not leave Thesia because nobody thought to try. They left her because every attempt reached the living floor through another person's body.

The guard stopped shouting. Darran arrived at the hatch and ordered the baffle cycle stabilized. He asked how many had been lost before he asked for a name.

“Thesia,” Mariya said. “One person. Write her name.”

He looked at the severed rope. “The ledge was not an exit.”

“We know that now.”

He closed the hatch. Mariya thought he would reduce the ration count by one before dusk. She would make him say the name again if he did.

***

By evening, forty-seven people remained. Mariya made herself count them, then read Thesia's name from the tile even though everyone had heard her say it. Pehl asked whether they would try the recess again. Annet said no before Mariya could answer. Orran said they should wait until the fields were mapped. None of them used the phrase *strongest first*.

Lios kept Thesia's strip of cloth at the sharp edge of the shelf. He asked whether her sister would want it. Mariya said she did not know. He folded it and placed it beside the cup until the message could go. Thesia had marked sleeping places in a room designed to make people stay put. The marks were still there, and several people followed them that night because they had learned where the water could be reached without crossing a bright line.

“The test found a landing,” Mariya said. “It also found a ledge that fails and a line that pulls at the rest of us. We won't send anyone across it as we are.”

She could see disappointment gather. A failed route had been a hope for a day; taking it away meant asking forty-seven people to sleep inside a system that called them necessary weight. She would rather disappoint them with a reason than promise a path she could not keep.

Nerissa touched her shoulder. “Let Orran do the night count. I can watch Annet's arm. Eat.”

Mariya meant to refuse. Then she heard Lios ask Pehl whether Thesia's sister would know. She sat down to eat because the group needed someone capable of speaking when the relay opened, and exhaustion would not make her truer.

The wafer lit shortly after dark. Mariya recorded the count and the tested limits: forty-seven alive, one dead; drinking water low; rations delivered but poor; service recess usable as a landing, impassable under the current tether; ninth-step click pattern unchanged. She named Thesia and asked that her sister be told the children had been fed. She did not send Tobious a command to save her. He would already be trying.

A small mark crossed the wafer when the packet left. Ypin had warned her what it meant: the relay had accepted it. It was not proof that anyone had heard her voice.

She told the captives exactly that. Then, seeing their faces, she added, “We should hear something by the next interval.”

Nerissa waited until the others had settled. “Should we?”

Mariya looked at the small mark, already fading. “I don't know.”

“Then why did you tell them?”

“Because they needed a time they could endure to.”

“And when it passes?”

Mariya had no answer. The wall clicked again, one high note and two low, counting a cycle that would continue whether she had promised anything or not.
