# SCHLELBERG

## Chapter Seventeen — The Terms of Mercy

“Yes,” Panjios said. “They were alive.”

The answer came before Tobious had time to dread an evasion. It did not make him feel better.

Panjios stood by the workbench with the old order between his hands. Twelve years earlier, a paired route had begun to fail while pressure built toward a populated service ward in Veyr. The ancient enclosure held ninety-seven people. If the route remained open, Suss's field model predicted a breach into the ward. If it was sealed, the people in the enclosure would be left with the load they were carrying. Panjios had signed the closure.

He showed Tobious the line he had signed. The public record had called the action an isolation of damaged material. The private order used the same phrase. No one had forged his hand. It was his judgment, and he had allowed the words to conceal what he knew from the service view.

“How much time did you have?” Tobious asked.

“Less than one hour from the first ward alarm. We spent most of it confirming the direction of the breach. By the time the closure reached my desk, the forecast said minutes.”

“Could you have moved them?”

“Not through the route as it stood. I did not test whether another access could take them. I believed testing would endanger the ward.”

Tobious wanted him to say there had been no other access. Panjios refused to give him that comfort. The record proved what he had chosen, not that he had exhausted every possibility. It was the same distance between urgency and knowledge that Tobious had crossed with Severance at the Storehouse.

“Did you see them?” Tobious asked.

“Some. Through the service view. I had their number.”

“You had their number.”

Panjios accepted the repetition. “I had no names.”

“You could have asked.”

“I could have. I believed the time it took would put the ward at risk. I signed without them.”

Ypin stood by the door, keeping the authenticated record in his hand. Tobious remembered Panjios standing between a spear and his body in the old chamber, insisting that another death would not bring back the guard's brother. That care had happened. So had this order. Tobious had wanted one fact to cancel the other because living with both was harder.

“And afterward?” he asked. “The report said zero people abandoned.”

“It said zero protected people. I let it stand. I told myself the decision had saved more lives and that publishing the other count would make us hesitate next time.”

“Did it?”

“It made the next time easier to conceal.”

Panjios turned the workroom lamp toward the signed page. “Afterward I told myself that if the model had been wrong, we would have saved the ward for nothing. I could not bear that possibility. So I spoke of the lives I had protected and let the others become a load in the official account. Each time I repeated it, I made it harder for someone else to challenge the next order.”

Tobious looked at Ypin. Ypin was reading the page, not Panjios's face. Neither man asked Tobious to call the admission courage. It had come twelve years late and only after he brought them the record.

The older man looked directly at him. The admission was not a request to be forgiven. Tobious was still angry that it had taken the finding of a record to draw it out.

“You knew this while you taught me to ask who pays for a cut.”

“Yes.”

“Why not tell me before I accepted Severance?”

Panjios's left knee shifted beneath him. “I feared you would refuse to work with us. I believed I could keep you from repeating what I did if I taught you the rule first.”

“You let me trust you without knowing what you had chosen.”

“I did.”

Tobious stepped away from the bench. He wanted to leave the room and force Panjios to follow, to have the argument somewhere wide enough for anger. He stayed because Mariya and forty-six others were under a field that would not wait for him to finish being hurt.

“I'll use what you know,” he said. “I won't give you the right to decide what I should be told.”

“That is fair.”

“It is not about fairness to you. If I have to stand at another cut, the people it reaches will not be protected by your hope that I ask the right question.”

Panjios bowed his head once. It was not an oath Tobious had asked for. It was the only reply he trusted at that moment.

***

Ypin brought the current load traces to Edda's calibration station. For weeks the stress had risen in short peaks, with less quiet time between them. A second series came from clinic observations: patients in the lower service ward with headaches, balance failures, and shield interference whenever the root load climbed. Edda had recorded the symptoms before anyone had asked her to prove a route was failing.

The two series were not identical. They did not need to be. Their peaks matched often enough that Ypin could test a forecast against injuries that had not been entered by command staff.

Edda brought them to the next room before she accepted the forecast. A transit mechanic sat with his eyes closed, waiting for the nausea from the last root fluctuation to pass. He had been working in a tunnel whose lights remained on; the floor had tilted in his body before any instrument announced a fault. Edda asked his permission to repeat the questions she had asked at intake. Tobious listened to the mechanic describe the hour and the service level. Ypin marked them on his trace. The match was not perfect. It was close enough to make another cycle of delay a risk to a person Tobious had now met.

“Suss will show the city a ward like this,” Edda said after the mechanic left. “He will be right that the root protects it. We need to say what our plan does to him when the root goes dark.”

“He can move to a conventional line?” Tobious asked.

“If the line is made ready and if someone tells him which tunnel not to enter while the transfer happens. Those are separate jobs.”

“The last viable disconnection window ends on Day Eighty-four,” he said. “After that, a full overlap can begin before a safe isolation.”

He put the word from the old maintenance records beside the forecast: **SCHLELBERG**. Tobious remembered Ypin first saying it over a map of two shores. Then it had sounded like a name for a fault someone would fix. Here it crossed a timetable of injured people, failed cooling lines, and captives holding a floor in place. It named the process by which neither era could remain whole if the root kept borrowing from the other.

“That is not eighty-four days from today,” Tobious said.

“No. From the first opening at Aras. We have thirty-seven.”

He put down three stones for the ancient accesses and one for the root. The tethered captives sat, in the model, on the branch beneath the Storehouse stone. To transfer Tobious's reference to another person, all three accesses had to remain idle for seven uninterrupted days. The current Storehouse line could not be made idle while forty-seven people carried its field. Shutting it around them would crush the enclosure or abandon them to the Iwang.

Tobious lifted the Salt stone and set it farther from the others. “What if I stay on this side and someone carries a regulator through Salt?”

Ypin returned the stone. “Salt is an access, not a separate root. The idle condition is at the reference. Moving a part through a gate changes where the part is. It does not change what the captives are attached to.”

“What if we cut Quarry first?”

“We can isolate a damaged access. The root will still carry Storehouse. The seven days still do not begin.”

He asked because every stone looked movable on the table. Ypin answered without making the questions sound foolish. By the end the four stones were where they had begun, and Tobious understood that a map made of small objects could be cruelly honest about distance.

“Then cut the root now,” Tobious said. “Destroy what keeps them there.”

“The same root holds their floor in its present state,” Panjios answered. “A terminal cut before release would turn their room into a collapse.”

Edda moved the future city layer over the model. Cooling and transit services had been built around the root's supply over generations. They could be separated, but not by a man swinging a sword without warning the households above. If the root went dark at once, some services would fail with people inside them.

Tobious looked at her. “You mean a permanent cut can be survived.”

“If we prepare,” Edda said. “And if the ancient people are out first. I have not yet seen a plan that does both by the window Ypin measured.”

Panjios pointed out the troughs between load peaks. They were narrowing. No one could tell them yet whether one would be long enough to release every captive, but there was a pattern to measure. Mariya's baffle notes might help them find where the load moved within it. The calendar was made of stresses and repair times, not an omen. That made it less forgiving; it also meant they could test it.

“You knew the terminal mode existed,” Tobious said to Panjios.

“Yes. We have not made the ancient release or the civic preparation possible.”

“Suss wants to keep the root.”

“He believes its loss would leave this city weaker than its enemies and its own shortages. He is right about the damage. He is wrong to decide the people under Aras can be kept there to avoid it.”

Tobious let the words settle. He had once thought an epic blade would make a hard choice simple. It could cut a root. It could not build beds for a city or carry forty-seven people up a broken stair.

***

Suss arrived with a proposal already measured. He showed Tobious a controlled high-gain trial, two guarded cuts in quick succession on an isolated branch, followed by a limited extraction if the trial held. He named the risk to Tobious's hand and nerves. He named the temporary rise in network load. He did not name the bodies to which that load could be routed.

He placed a second forecast beside Ypin's. Both ended near Day Eighty-four. Suss's line was narrower because his command gauges did not include Edda's injury reports; Ypin's was rougher because he lacked the protected junction data. The agreement between them frightened Tobious more than either forecast alone. Suss had not come to dismiss the deadline. He had come with a way to use it.

“The public will move service wards if you ask them to,” Suss said to Edda. “They will not dismantle a functioning root because you cannot promise the ancient floor will survive another week.”

“I have not asked them to dismantle it today,” Edda answered. “I am asking you to count the people on that floor as patients when you measure harm.”

Suss looked at Tobious. “You know what happens when we let an unknown load remain unknown until the time to act is gone.”

Tobious did. He had made a cut too soon. Suss was pressing the opposite fear: that asking one more question could become an excuse to do nothing. Both fears had a real history now. Neither named the people who would absorb this trial's heat.

“The first extraction can give us direct evidence of the far floor,” Suss said. “We need it to plan the rest.”

“You offered to take Mariya,” Tobious replied. “She hasn't agreed.”

“Then speak to her.”

Tobious looked at the diagram. The gain setting was the one he had used in the isolated test after the cooling-station rescue. It had worked. Its heat had appeared on a gauge, not on a person. This branch was different. Ypin's records left part of its routing behind command permission.

“I want every measure shown to Ypin,” he said. “And Edda sees my medical readings. I will not carry a setting whose only warning is that I fall over afterward.”

Suss agreed to the operator readings. Panjios objected that the network route remained unverified. Suss answered that no extraction would be authorized until a trial had reduced that uncertainty. Tobious could see where Panjios was right. He could also hear the ninety-seven unnamed people inside every warning the older man now gave. Losing trust did not change the engineering; it made Tobious demand independent proof that was not yet available.

“A guarded trial,” he said. “Not a live extraction until we hear from Mariya.”

“Agreed,” Suss said. “A supervised diagnostic window opens tomorrow. You can speak with her.”

Tobious put his name on the trial authorization. He read the lines Ypin translated about injury to the operator. He asked for a measure of the network strain and accepted Suss's promise to provide it. He did not ask what *network* meant at the far floor, or whether a monitor that called its people loads could tell him when one of them was hurt.

Ypin watched him sign. He had put his own name on a form without looking behind a category. He could have stopped Tobious by refusing to operate the trial. He did not yet have evidence that the isolated branch reached the captives. He did say, “I want the full return path before you touch it.”

“Suss said you'll have the readings.”

“Readings after the cut are not the same as a route before it.”

Tobious knew the difference. He had taught himself to know it at the debrief wall. He answered, “Then help me get the route,” but left the signed form on Suss's table. He had not made Ypin a condition of consent. He had asked him to repair a decision already taken.

The omission was not made for him. He made it himself because a working method and a narrowing window had offered him something that felt like control.
