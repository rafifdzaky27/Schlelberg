# SCHLELBERG

## Chapter Twenty-Eight — The Hollow Ones

At dawn the annex held forty-five living people in different stages of leaving it.

Some had passed the first tether and waited in the dry holding recess. Others still stood on the far floor, tied to a branch that brightened whenever someone shifted weight. Mariya moved between the two as the baffle allowed, her board pressed against her chest. Nerissa read each name at the point of release and again when that person reached the outer stair. A person was not safe because a line had been cut. They were safe when someone saw them beyond the next danger and had a place for them to rest.

The first man who reached the recess refused the blanket the outside bearers offered. He had watched the passage behind him and insisted his sister had not yet crossed. Mariya found her on the far floor, conscious but too weak to walk without a hand. She showed him the name beside hers on the board and did not ask him to trust ink over his own eyes. One of the carriers moved him to a place where he could see the stair. He took the blanket when his sister came through. A second woman wanted to go back for a clay figure her daughter had made while they were confined. The figure was behind an active line. Nerissa kept the woman at the recess while Mariya marked where it lay. If the field cleared, someone might retrieve it. No one would cross the line for a promise that could not keep them alive.

They had used the night to teach the route to people who had not heard the first briefing. Now the instructions came in fragments that a frightened person could use: *Wait at the cup. Hands on the strap. Stop when Jorra's palm opens.* Mariya heard those phrases pass from bearer to bearer. There were no speeches. Every repetition made it more likely that the next body would reach daylight.

Lios gave the agreed cup signal on the fourth bell. Nerissa watched the same sound from the opposite side of the recess. On one cycle he missed it; the Iwang clicking inside the wall had drawn his attention. Nerissa signaled in his place. He apologized. She told him the backup had worked as it was meant to.

Mariya heard the creatures before she saw them. Air passing through their open torsos made a low, empty note unlike a human breath. Pale struts pressed against the active seam. One body flattened so thin that the wall's old pattern showed through it, then gathered hard weight as its collar came through the field. Jorra's shield met the strike. The impact drove her toward the wall, and Sennos shifted the stretcher team away from the line of force.

The creature left no blood on the shield. Its pressure remained after it withdrew, a set of fine cracks climbing the plaster beside Jorra's arm. Mariya had seen one Iwang at the first opening months ago, when it was solid enough for a bronze point to wound at the collar. She had forgotten the sound of its weight returning. It was not a ghost slipping through stone; it had to borrow the thinness of an active boundary, then become a body again to strike. That rule gave the defenders a moment. It did not make the moment generous.

Jorra counted the next contraction aloud. Sennos moved with her rather than following the creature. Their shields overlapped only at the corner where the stretcher would turn. A second Iwang pressed at a farther seam but found no active passage and recoiled into its own narrow dark. Mariya could see only parts of the fight through the bearers and the stone. She did not need to see a victory. She needed the turn to stay open for the length of one patient.

“It has to take shape to reach us,” he called. “Watch the collar.”

Tobious was at the cut point, too far from Mariya for her to see the expression beneath the scar at his left eye. She could see the limit of his action. He had to hold Severance against a tether for three beats, lose his own protection, and wait afterward. Jorra and Sennos kept the Iwang from him while he did that work. The ancient workers moved people when the field let them. No single person commanded the whole passage.

The next group included Hessah on a stretcher. She had asked to keep the repaired water skin against her chest. Its red knot was still there. Her mother walked beside the bearers, carrying a second vessel in case the first leaked. At the wide turn, the bearer on the wall side adjusted his grip exactly as Hessah had shown him in their test. She closed her eyes through the jolt but did not cry out. They reached the holding recess.

The bearer was the young man who had objected to the wider route because of Thesia's death. His palm came away from the brace white with dust. He checked whether the lower wedge had shifted before he stepped across. It had not. Hessah saw him checking and said, without opening her eyes, “You can tell me when you're frightened. I already am.”

“I'm frightened,” he said.

“Good. Keep looking.”

Mariya almost smiled. Hessah had been making jokes about broken water skins when the rest of them could scarcely stand. This was not a last speech. It was how she had learned to keep people useful to one another in a room that gave them very little else.

“Water,” she said.

Her mother gave her a measured sip. Mariya noted the stop and the time on her board. They had planned rest places because courage could not stand in for a body that needed one.

***

Darran came to the baffle control with two guards after the second morning group cleared.

He had changed his cloak since Mariya saw him at the ration hatch. It was clean at the shoulder and muddy at the hem. He had come quickly from the surface, but not so quickly that he had forgotten the seal of his office. One guard wore a worker's cloth over his mouth. The other held a spear with both hands and kept glancing at the men carrying his own village's wounded.

“You've changed the sequence,” he said. He saw the service panel open and the copied latch in Orran's hand. “Close that. The root has to remain balanced.”

“The people are coming out,” Mariya answered. “You can see them.”

“I can see what happens to the site when the load falls.”

“You can see who has been holding it,” Mariya said. She pointed to the people waiting in the recess. “You have had their names for weeks.”

“I have had figures that changed every time you sent a packet.”

“People died. The count changed because they died.”

For a moment Darran looked toward the stretcher. Hessah's mother stood between him and her daughter without raising a weapon. The guard with the spear lowered its point a hand's breadth. Mariya hoped Darran might hear the difference between a falling load and a life being carried out. He was already turning to the control.

He had spent the summer keeping them fed just enough to work, then calling the result a salvage operation. If the Receiver failed now, his commission would be measured by an empty ruin and the names he had hidden. He pushed past his guard and tried to restore the baffle's old pattern.

“Stop,” Orran said. “There are bearers in that turn.”

Darran threw the control. The seam changed color. The Iwang that had been striking Jorra's field lost its old line of approach and followed the newly energized baffle toward the control station. Its limbs flattened along the wall, passed a thinned boundary, and became solid where Darran stood. He had time to look at the dark nexus beneath its collar. He put up one arm. The creature struck him and drew back into the current. The control fell from his hand.

The spear guard lunged too late. His bronze point struck the creature's collar as it turned and came away with a dark flake on the tip. The Iwang had already folded back into the boundary. He stared at the spear, then at Darran on the floor. Mariya told him to help carry the living. He looked at the second guard; neither had received an order from Darran to permit it. The second guard went to the nearest stretcher. The first followed.

Mariya did not feel victory. Darran's attempt had shifted the load while people were still on the old passage. His death gave them no safe interval; it left a baffle in the wrong position.

“Hold the brace,” Orran called to the stoneworkers. “Get the stretcher past me.”

The wooden support beneath the wide turn had cracked when the field changed. Orran set his shoulder to it and took the weight while the bearers carried the next person across. He had agreed to that position after hearing the risk, with two others to relieve him. The first reached him. The second could not get through the narrowing gap while a stretcher was on it.

Orran had carved a shallow mark in the brace at the place where he wanted the relief worker to put a hand. The first worker found it and pushed. For one breath Orran could ease his shoulder. Then the far edge slipped and the weight returned, more heavily. He called the changed pressure to Mariya, not to frighten the bearers but because a plan could not be corrected if the person at its most dangerous point hid what he felt.

The patient on the stretcher asked them to set him down. There was no stable floor under the middle of the turn. Mariya told him where the next stone was and asked whether he could keep his hands inside the frame. He nodded. She could not see whether he believed they would reach it. She made the bearers wait for the shield overlap, then sent them the remaining steps.

“Leave the board,” Mariya said. “Move back.”

“The board is under them.”

The person on the stretcher was awake, gripping the sides. Tobious moved from the cut point with his blade sheathed. Another worker brought a wedge. They tried to shift the weight off Orran's shoulder so he could step clear. The first attempt lifted the brace a fraction. Orran turned to take the offered hand.

The field surged through the damaged support. Stone broke above the wedge. Tobious's shield caught the first fragments, but the main beam struck the place where Orran stood. He disappeared from Mariya's view behind dust and the falling edge of the board.

The bearer nearest him tried to go back. Jorra caught the bearer by the strap and stopped him before his foot crossed the live seam. Tobious and the worker had the safer angle from the outer side. The bearer shouted Orran's name once, furious at being held. Mariya could not tell him to be calm. She told him to keep the stretcher level while the people who could reach Orran tried.

Tobious and the worker pulled the board clear. They reached for Orran. Nerissa came from the holding recess and stopped beside them; her face gave Mariya the answer before she spoke. The stretcher had passed. Orran had not.

“Forty-four,” Nerissa said when she could make herself count. She did not say the number as praise for the rescue. She said it so the next group would be carried by the people who remained.

Mariya wrote Orran's name beneath the others. She remembered him arguing that the strong should leave first. He had changed his mind in the work itself, not in a speech designed to make his death useful. His life did not become worth less because he had been afraid before.

The baffle could not be restored from Darran's control without exposing the corridor again. Ypin's relay offered a safer route through a slower interval. Mariya and Tobious chose it in front of the bearers. No one cut another tether until every person still on the damaged turn had been moved to stable stone.

***

By afternoon the outer drainage slope was full of villagers carrying water, stretchers, and names. Samon sat where the old channel met the yard, telling each group which stones would take a wheel and which would collapse beneath one. Therren's soldiers kept the road clear after the captain saw Darran's body carried out; none tried to reclaim the workers at the stair. The coastal carriers had shelter ready beyond the slope.

The soldier with Darran's spear came out bearing a water vessel for a woman he did not know. Nobody praised him. At the cart turn, a carrier asked him to put down the spear before he took hold of a patient. He did. The weapon stayed against the wall until someone claimed it for the ridge patrol. Mariya watched the exchange because it was smaller than peace and larger than an order.

Nerissa had made the outside workers leave a space between the two carts where a person could be examined out of the crush. She sent one child there whose breathing had turned shallow. The child recovered after sitting and drinking slowly. Another man with a bruised ankle insisted he could help unload a stretcher. She gave him a roll of cloth to sort while seated. The work mattered. So did the fact that she would not let him prove usefulness by injuring himself further.

Mariya came through with the last group. She had counted forty-four after Orran. Nerissa counted again at the outer gate, matching each person to a household or a named carer. Lios reached his aunt and handed her the repaired cup. She put a hand around its rim as if testing whether it was real, then drew him against her.

Hessah reached open air on the final stretcher. Her mother asked for the planned rest place, and Mariya made the bearers stop beneath a cloth shade. Edda's instructions had come through the relay; Nerissa followed what she could use with the water and medicines they had. They warmed Hessah, eased the bindings that pressed on her chest, and gave her a sip when she asked. The skin with the red knot lay beside her hand.

Her mother wanted the sea wind brought to her. A carrier loosened the shade on the side facing the shore, then held it when the cloth snapped too hard. The breeze was still coming over the fields. Hessah reached for the red knot, found it, and ran a finger along the seam she had repaired herself. The skin had survived the passage. Mariya wished she could count that as evidence her maker would, too.

Nerissa listened at Hessah's chest and asked her where the pain was strongest. Hessah answered once. When she could no longer give a full answer, Nerissa did not turn her into an object of whispered estimates. She told her mother what she was doing and why. She told Hessah as well. Mariya helped hold the shade while they eased her breathing. The work was small and exact, and it was all they had.

“I thought the air would smell of salt,” Hessah said.

“The wind is inland,” her mother answered. “It will turn.”

Mariya wanted to say the city beyond the sea could heal her. There was not enough time to take her through Salt and back, and no assurance that advanced care could undo weeks of injury to a body already failing. She helped Nerissa keep Hessah comfortable while they waited for a stronger breath. It did not come.

Hessah died where she could see the sky. Her mother held the repaired water skin. Mariya wrote the fifth captive loss beneath Thesia, Pehl, Annet, and Orran. Forty-three of the original forty-eight lived, including Mariya. Darran had never belonged on the captives' roll. The twelve freed on the first day had their own record, kept by the shelter households.

Her mother asked Mariya to write that Hessah had reached the open air. Then she shook her head. “No. Write where she died. I will remember what she saw.”

Mariya wrote the place. She had learned to give the dead truthful records instead of kinder inventions. The mother kept the red knot in her hand while the living were counted again.

The roll took time. A man had gone to a neighboring shelter and had to be found there before his name was marked safe. One child had fallen asleep under a cart. Mariya did not close the list until each survivor had someone who knew where they would wake.

At last she stood on the slope. Aras spread above her, damaged and still inhabited. People were arguing over how to move a stretcher through a narrow lane; someone else was bringing more water. The open sky was not a reward for all the dead. It was room for the living to work in, and she could feel both truths as the wind finally turned from the sea.
