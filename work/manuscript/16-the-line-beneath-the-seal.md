# SCHLELBERG

## Chapter Sixteen — The Line Beneath the Seal

Ypin could open the maintenance archive because the Storehouse fault still shared an index with older route failures. He could not open the command chamber beyond it. The distinction was written beside the door in letters Tobious could now recognize as a warning but still could not read fast enough to obey without help.

“If this page asks for a supervisor, I stop,” Ypin said. “I will not make the record less useful by breaking into something I cannot explain later.”

“You said you wanted enough proof for Edda.”

“Enough. Not a story I make whole by guessing at locked parts.”

The archive was cool, crowded with narrow racks of memory wafers. Maintenance logs opened on a table when Ypin set an access tag against its edge. Each route had a date, a service request, and a disposal code. Most were dull: worn seals, cooling faults, workers who left a job half-finished for the next shift. Tobious had spent enough days beside Ypin to know that dull records were what kept the city from falling. A strange code hidden among them was more frightening than an obvious warning.

Ypin made him take a second chair and read the public digest while Ypin checked the maintenance index. The digest showed bridges kept open, crews counted, strain reduced to a tolerable level. Tobious could now pick out some words before the translator caught up: *safe, completed, no protected loss.* He had used those words himself after the twelve came out. He had meant them to describe what happened. They described only the part of it he could bear to present.

At the next rack, a woman from the archive staff was repairing a bent reader pin. She asked whether they wanted the bridge display with the original sound. Ypin said they needed the service index instead. She directed them to a lower shelf where older formats were still kept because converting them would erase calibration marks. She had no reason to think the request unusual. Tobious watched her return to the pin. A room could hold a hidden catastrophe and ordinary work at the same time.

They searched the date of the evacuation shown in the memorial gallery. Tobious remembered the bridge bearer's repeated turn of the head. Panjios had said the old inscription left out the maintenance crews. Ypin found their report, then followed a reference to an earlier closure in the same route family, twelve paired years before the current crisis.

The old route had been repaired twice after its closure. The first entry listed a cracked seal. The second listed a need to keep the ancient branch cold while the future service line remained useful. Neither repair reopened the isolated side. Ypin traced the work orders with his finger. A closure he had imagined as one terrible day had demanded workers, parts, and power long afterward. Someone had kept it stable because it still served the city.

“This isn't the bridge,” Tobious said.

“No. Same service index. Look at the load line.”

The screen showed ninety-seven unregistered biological loads on the ancient side. The official rescue report beneath it showed zero protected persons abandoned. The second number had been printed in the public memorial digest. It did not lie about the category. It erased what the category allowed the public to believe.

Tobious asked Ypin to show the life readings before the seal. They rose and fell in separate rhythms. A person shifting position sent a small wave across one line; another settled when the cooling flow changed. Ninety-seven was a count, not ninety-seven names, yet the lines resisted being made into one gray band. Tobious did not know who any of them had been. He knew that the report's zero was not a report of an empty room.

Ypin opened the isolation order. A seal had cut the route while the ninety-seven were still registered as active loads. The record did not show their names. It did show pressure afterward and the absence of further life readings on that branch.

“Were they people?” Tobious asked.

“The service flags describe respiration and neural response. So yes.”

“Then the report says no one was left.”

“It says no protected person was left. I don't want to make that distinction for them. I want to show where they made it.”

Ypin saved the maintenance entry under its original authentication. He could not alter its author without leaving a trace. He could carry an excerpt to Edda; the full command context remained behind another door. When Tobious asked whether they could publish it now, Ypin held up the copy.

The archive worker returned with a packet of fresh reader sleeves. She saw the figures on their table and asked whether they had found a calibration fault. Ypin covered the current manifest with his hand, then removed it. “We found a count filed under the wrong public category,” he said. “I need to preserve the access record.”

She showed him how to mark the timestamp and sign as custodian of the copy. She did not ask whom the old count belonged to. Her job gave them a reliable trail, and Tobious noticed how much the truth would depend on a worker who had not entered this room to take anyone's side.

“We can prove a route was closed on live loads and that the public count omitted them. We cannot yet prove who knew an alternative existed. If we claim more than this record shows, they will spend the inquiry tearing that claim apart.”

Tobious thought of the forty-seven current captives. They did not have years for an inquiry. Yet a rushed accusation might let a practiced commander call the whole case reckless, including the parts that were true. He wanted the evidence to strike like Severance. It was going to require more hands.

***

The name they were looking for was in the authorization block.

Tobious recognized its shape before the translator spoke: Panjios. The old signature had been pressed beneath a directive to isolate the route. Suss's field command counter-sign followed on the implementation page. Tobious read the two lines three times, waiting for a word that would make them mean something else.

“Panjios told us a bearer kept the bridge open,” he said.

“Different incident,” Ypin replied. “He has told us very little about this one.”

There was a note after the isolation order: review deferred under continuing emergency authority. It had been renewed, then filed closed. Tobious saw how a decision made in a crisis could become a category, and a category a habit. The old ninety-seven were not in the room with Mariya. They did not change the current forty-seven-person count. They had been removed from a count in a way that made removing the next people easier.

Ypin sat very still. He had opened a newer form attached to the same classification code. Its lower field bore his own signature.

He turned the form so Tobious could read the field he had signed. The route number was small. The supply quantities were large. A normal replacement order for a regulator that might have kept the tether stable, carried inside the same category that had hidden the older loss.

“When?” Tobious asked.

“Eighteen months ago. It was a routine manifest. I checked the weight and the cooling draw. I signed because the values matched.”

“Did you know?”

“I knew the name of the category. I thought it meant biological samples, waste cultures, whatever a station held that needed a stable environment.” He rubbed a hand across his face. “I didn't ask what the people were moving.”

“You asked me for every reading before I made a cut,” Tobious said.

“Yes.”

“You knew to ask.”

Ypin looked at the old date, then at his own. “I learned to ask when a sword was in the room. I should have learned to ask when I signed for the power that let someone else use one.”

Tobious thought of Ypin teaching him to eat the soft grain cakes with a sharp relish, of the night they had repaired a small pump until the water ran clear. Those evenings had been real. So was this form. Friendship did not turn the signature into a harmless mistake; it made the work of looking at it together harder.

Tobious had wanted a guilty man to point at. Ypin had given him one, smaller than Suss, more reachable, and standing beside him now. He remembered his own hand on Severance at the Storehouse. Knowing the count had not kept him from moving a cost onto other people.

“We need your signature in the copy,” Tobious said.

“I know.”

Ypin attached the newer form. His name would travel with it to Edda. He did not ask Tobious to understand him first.

He put the copy in a sleeve that could be opened only by breaking the seal. Then he made a second copy for the review office and logged where both would go. Tobious helped him carry the packets. It was a smaller burden than the one under Aras, but he could feel the wish to leave Ypin alone with it. He kept walking beside him.

***

They crossed the memorial passage on the way to Panjios's workroom. The bridge bearer still turned her head toward whatever lay outside the image. Tobious stopped beneath the wall. Ypin's request to add the omitted maintenance crews had acquired a reply: denied until the committee could verify a complete roster. A precise reason. An indefinite wait.

Tobious watched the loop again. A child at the next display asked why the bearer kept looking away from the bridge. Her father said she was checking whether everyone had crossed. The answer was plausible; the public caption said as much. Tobious had no proof what she had actually seen outside the frame. He wondered how many visitors had left this hall carrying the same comforting account. He did not correct the father with a different guess. The missing crews could be named when Ypin found their roster. The ninety-seven needed an account from the man who had signed.

“She deserves to be remembered,” Tobious said.

“So do they,” Ypin answered.

Tobious carried the authenticated excerpts under his coat. He did not bring Severance. It remained in its controlled case after training, where it belonged while he asked a man for an account of what he had done with authority.

Panjios's door stood half-open. Through it Tobious could see a bench of service tools, a brace for the older man's stiff knee, a bowl with the remains of a meal he had eaten at his desk. None of the ordinary things absolved him. They made it harder to arrange the question as though a monster sat inside the room.

On the bench lay the dark practice blade Panjios had used to show Tobious a shield at the village terrace. Its edge was blunt and its field inactive. Panjios had once said Tobious could not be strong enough to stand wherever he wished, and had taught him to choose a place someone else could cover. Tobious had accepted the lesson from a man whose own signature now lay under his coat. The skill had saved lives. The teacher had also helped conceal a loss. Tobious could not decide which fact he wanted to make the other disappear.

Panjios looked up when they entered. He saw the old date on the first page and set down his stylus. Tobious watched his face closely. Panjios did not feign surprise. He looked at the second page, saw Suss's counter-sign, and closed his eyes for the length of one breath.

“You found it,” he said.

“We found the load count,” Tobious answered. “Ninety-seven. The public report says zero.”

“It says zero protected losses,” Panjios replied.

“You know why that difference matters.”

“I do.”

There was no practiced denial in his voice. Tobious found he had prepared himself for one and had no better opening when it failed to come. Ypin set the sealed packets where Panjios could see the authentication marks without touching them.

Panjios looked at Ypin. “What else?”

“The route's life readings stopped after isolation. Your authorization. Suss's implementation. We have no testimony from the ancient side.”

Panjios nodded. The accuracy of the answer seemed to hurt him more than an accusation would have.

Tobious laid the papers on the workbench, where the light was good enough for all three of them to see the signatures.

“Were they alive when you closed it?” he asked.
