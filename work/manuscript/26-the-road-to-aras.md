# SCHLELBERG

## Chapter Twenty-Six — The Road to Aras

The message from Veyr took nearly the whole diagnostic window to play.

Mariya listened once with Nerissa, Orran, and two other council members beside her. Ypin's voice named the low-load interval on Days Eighty-two and Eighty-three. Jorra's voice described cover at the wider service recess. Edda gave the number of people a stretcher team could carry before changing bearers. Tobious spoke last. He said the final cut would destroy the root, every ancient access with it, and the material in Severance's core. He would have to make that cut in Veyr on Day Eighty-four. Once it was done, he could not come back.

Mariya asked the wafer to replay that part. It did. The last words were no softer the second time.

“Can he change the reference before then?” she asked into the relay.

Ypin's recorded answer followed, anticipating the question: seven uninterrupted idle days with all receivers quiet. The captives could not safely be released before the trough. Once they were out, only hours remained. There would be no time to transfer the reference.

“Can I cross?” she asked during the brief live return channel.

Ypin answered at once. “Yes. You can come to Veyr through Salt before the closure if you choose. It is not the machine that keeps you in Aras.”

The line went quiet as the window ended. Mariya looked at the people waiting around her. The release sequence mattered to all of them; her future residence did not need to be decided in front of forty-five frightened people. She told the council she accepted the proposed rescue in principle and wanted one day to consider her own crossing. Nerissa took the wafer and began repeating the practical sections for those who had missed a word.

Orran asked why the cut began at the far branch instead of the one nearest the door. Nerissa found the passage in Ypin's recording: beginning near the door would leave the stretchers farther in carrying the released load. Hessah asked where a person would wait if the first shield failed. Jorra had marked the dry recess; Mariya showed it on the sketch and admitted that no shield would make the passage harmless. A woman whose brother had been one of the twelve already freed asked whether the outside team had told those twelve to remain beyond the old watercourse. They had. Their safety was one reason the returning workers could not use the fastest approach.

The council did not vote as if fear could be made unanimous. They named the people who could not be moved without a stretcher, the volunteers who wanted to remain last, and the conditions under which Mariya or Nerissa would halt the cut. One young carrier wanted the group to wait for a route with no Iwang at all. Mariya let him finish. Then she showed him the narrowing trough and asked him to help choose a real waiting place for the first stretcher. He did, still afraid. She was relieved he had not pretended otherwise.

Later, in the dry recess, Mariya sat beside Nerissa with Hessah's repaired water skin between them. She tried to imagine Veyr without making it only Tobious's city. He had described towers where food grew in controlled beds, streets hanging above the bay, a clinic that could see bones without cutting skin. There would be work there. She might learn to read the fast-changing signs; she might use what she knew of stores to help people whose systems had different names for hunger. She might walk beside Tobious at dusk and not hear the baffle click through a wall.

She imagined a morning in a room no neighbor knew. Tobious would go to work and she would buy food under a roof that had never held a harvest. She could ask questions without someone saying what her mother would have done. She could be foolish in public and not be measured against the girl who once miscounted a wedding store. That freedom drew her almost as much as the thought of sleeping beside him. It frightened her because it was hers. No one had ordered her to want it.

Then she imagined the shore road after rain, the brown runnels meeting the tide. She had argued with Orran at that road over whose stones held the wall; she had once carried a sick child along it while Nerissa came from the other direction. She wanted a life in which those memories were neither a chain nor a relic. The village could change while she remained in it. If she left, it would change without her, and she might still be happy elsewhere. Both lives had a cost. She would not tell herself that only one of them was honest.

“I could want that life,” she said.

“Of course you could,” Nerissa answered.

“If I stay, people will say I was needed here.”

“You are. So are other people. We have begun to prove that.”

Mariya looked toward the narrow line of daylight at the far stair. Beyond it lay the sea she had known since childhood, a mill that annoyed her, houses whose people remembered her mistakes as clearly as her help. Staying would not restore the old Aras. She did not want the old Aras back in full; it had been possible for a widow to vanish from its food list before any Telassari scribe arrived. She wanted to help make a different home on the same shore.

“Then I will choose it because I want to live there,” she said, “not because you would all fail without me.”

Nerissa adjusted the water skin's red knot. “Tell Tobious that part yourself.”

“He might offer me a place again.”

“He probably will.”

“I want him to.” Mariya stared at the knot. “I want to know the offer is real, even if I refuse it.”

Nerissa gave the water skin back to Hessah's mother when she came to fetch it. Neither woman asked Mariya to decide faster. At the ration bell, Mariya went with the others and took her share. She had made choices while hungry before. This one deserved to be made after she ate.

***

Darran's new ration order reached the annex the next morning. Food would enter only with approved technicians. The weak would receive it last because, he said, the walking workers had to remain productive. Mariya showed the guard the last recorded meal tally and the discrepancies Darran had not corrected. If the count he submitted to Therren was examined, the missing portions would point to his supervision. He could keep the ration hatch narrow; he could not make the figures disappear.

He had brought a clerk to witness the new order. The clerk looked from Darran's seal to Mariya's tally and tried to hold both slates without touching the damp wall. “The living count is forty-five?” he asked.

“Yes,” Mariya said.

“The delivery list is thirty-eight.”

Darran took back his slate. “Some are not assigned to work.”

“People who cannot lift stone still drink,” Nerissa said. She stood behind Mariya with a water vessel that was empty enough for the clerk to hear it.

The clerk did not endorse Mariya's larger claim. He wrote the discrepancy and made Darran initial it. That small mark changed Darran's face. He could threaten the annex in private; he could not now claim he had never been shown how many mouths his order had omitted.

“What do you want?” Darran asked.

“A supervised water transfer. We have people who cannot carry it from the inner hatch. Let Orran and two others bring it through the service panel.”

“The panel isn't for laborers.”

“Then send your guard to watch them. The people you need alive will drink.”

He allowed one transfer, intending to keep the workers visible. During it, Orran pressed wax against the ordinary latch beneath the panel. Mariya stood between him and the guard, arguing over the volume of water, a dispute both men expected from her. Orran withdrew the wax impression under his thumb. Later a stoneworker outside would copy the simple shape into a service tool. It would open one panel. It would not undo the temporal tethers or replace Severance.

The first vessel struck the frame as Orran lifted it through. A bead of wax slid toward the guard's boot. Orran caught it with the wet edge of his thumb before it fell. Mariya raised the empty vessel and demanded the rest of the allotted water. The guard turned to Darran for the number; Darran had to consult the clerk's altered slate. In that brief quarrel Orran pressed the impression into a fold of cloth and passed it to a bearer on the near side. No one opened the panel again. Their advantage depended on a single use, and Mariya could already feel how easily it might have been lost for the sake of one careless lift.

Lios watched the ration bell from the dry recess. He raised his cup on the fourth strike, then lowered it when Nerissa raised a hand beside him. The adult backup heard the same bell. They had given him a part that mattered and an exit from it if fear or noise made him miss the cue.

Darran counted the empty water vessels before leaving. He noticed nothing changed in the panel. Mariya knew he would tighten inspections if the copied latch appeared. The advantage would need to be used once, at the trough, not practiced until he learned it.

***

The carrier network answered through a fisherman who had permission to bring salt to the outer store. He could not pass a letter to the captives, so he repeated what the neighboring households had agreed. Several villages would withhold harbor transport and grain carts unless Therren reduced the seasonal levy, released ordinary labor detainees, and withdrew his men from Aras's houses to the ridge. They would not attack the citadel. Their leverage was the work his soldiers needed to keep a district fed and supplied.

The fisherman had no promise that all the carriers would hold. One headman had a son in a Telassari work camp and feared what a transport stoppage would do to him. Another had agreed to stop grain carts only after the current loads reached a hospital. Mariya asked the fisherman to repeat those limits as carefully as the demands. A coalition that lied about its own edges would fail the first time Therren pressed on one.

“Do they know about the people below?” she asked.

“They know some. They ask whether your list can be copied.”

She gave him the roll with the living named first and the dead afterward. She included the twelve freed earlier, in a separate column, so the number of captives could not be inflated by treating them as still trapped. A household whose daughter was among the twelve would know the difference. The fisherman read the marks once and tucked the roll under the false bottom of his salt basket. He had carried news before. This time he asked Mariya to say the names aloud so he would remember them if the paper were found.

Mariya sent back a count that could be checked: the captives still alive, the dead named separately, and the households whose workers had been taken. She asked the carriers to speak to Therren with those households present if the negotiation could be arranged. A victory announced by one courier would be another count no one else could inspect.

She also sent a message to Tobious. She supported the rescue and wanted a private moment with him after the last person was safe, if time allowed. She intended to remain in her own era. She would not ask him to leave the root unfinished, and she did not want him to describe her choice as a duty that proved her love.

Nerissa took the store list from her hands when the wafer confirmed delivery. “I will manage tomorrow's portions,” she said. “You can help. You do not have to be the only one who knows where everything is.”

Mariya let her take it. The surrender felt strange. It made space for a choice she could claim as a person rather than as the last support of a failing room.
