# SCHLELBERG

## Chapter Three — A Blade of Quiet Light

By morning, Tobious had learned which of the storehouse walls received the first heat of the sun. He had been awake long enough to watch its pale square of light travel over seven baskets of dried figs, a stack of broken jar lids, and the single sealed door.

The soldiers had taken his belt. They had left him his damp tunic because it was not worth having. His wrist had thickened during the night. Whenever he forgot and tried to use it, pain drew a sharp line up his arm.

Someone stopped outside the door. Tobious rose. It was a mistake; his knees had stiffened, and his shoulder struck the wall.

Darran came in carrying bread and a water bowl. He set both on a low crate just beyond Tobious's reach.

“You opened it by touching the green metal,” he said.

Tobious stared at the bowl. “How is Samon?”

“The leg is still attached.”

“That's not an answer.”

“It is the answer I have.” Darran drew the hide from its leather tube. In daylight it looked worse than the fragment Tobious had seen underground: old creases, a repair of neat stitches along one edge, several marks added in a different ink. “There was no command to put a hand inside the frame. What did you feel before the light?”

Tobious said nothing.

“If it opened once, it can open again.”

“A man died the last time.”

“Because a guard mistook something he did not understand for something he could stab.”

Tobious remembered the bronze tip finding the creature's collar. Without it, he and Lios might have been the ones left on the stairs.

“He hurt it,” he said. “For a moment.”

Darran looked down at his drawing. “Did you see what was beyond?”

“A passage.”

“Before that.”

Tobious moved away from the crate. The thing he had seen before the passage was a city and a man whose face had been his own. He had thought of that face all night. In the time it took to draw a breath, he could recall the cut beneath the left eye, the strange close-fitted clothes, blood escaping through a hand. He could not recall where the buildings had ended. Perhaps they had not.

“You knew what was there,” Tobious said.

“I knew enough to order you to clear it.”

“Your own hands were free.”

Darran's eyes lifted. His cheeks were marked by the narrow straps of a surveyor's leather cap. He wore no officer's bronze, but men with bronze had obeyed him below.

“Eat,” he said. “You'll need your strength.”

He left the bowl and bread on the crate as the door closed behind him.

Tobious waited until the steps had passed. Then he ate, because hunger could not punish Darran and would only weaken him.

***

The captain came shortly after noon. Captain Therren had removed his corselet. Without it, the man from the levy yard seemed narrower, but the effect vanished when he sat across from Tobious and asked how many workers the flooded chamber had required.

“Six,” Tobious said. “Not counting me.”

“How many did you send out?”

“I didn't. Darran did.”

Therren nodded to the scribe at his shoulder. The man made a mark in the clay.

“He says you broke the apparatus.”

“I broke the thing that held the doorway open.”

“The apparatus.”

“It was coming through.”

“And you acted without his order.”

Tobious looked at the clay tablet. A man could move the boundaries of an event merely by choosing where to press a stylus.

“Samon was hurt because he came back for me,” he said. “A guard died because Darran opened it again. You can put that beside my name.”

Therren did not glance at the scribe. “If I put every man's account beside his name, we would have no clay left before winter.”

“Then you're measuring with the wrong jar.”

The captain's mouth tightened, though Tobious could not tell whether he was hiding anger or something like amusement.

“You have a useful skill,” Therren said. “That's why you're sitting in a room with bread instead of beside the dead guard.”

There it was again: useful. It had saved him from a spear point in the granary yard, and it had brought him beneath the hill.

“Have Samon taken to Nerissa,” Tobious said. “She sets limbs. He needs clean water and people who know him.”

“He has been taken there.”

Tobious looked up.

“My men have to walk those stairs,” Therren said. “I want the man who knows the drain to survive. I want him capable of telling us whether the ground will go under our feet.”

It was not kindness. Tobious could use the result without mistaking it for kindness.

“How bad?”

“Bad enough that she asked for splints, oil, and two hands she trusts. I gave her the hands.”

Therren stood. The scribe followed. At the door, the captain paused.

“Someone who knows the device is coming. My commission is to deliver the site in working order. Darran's is to find it. Those are different obligations.”

“Who gave him his?”

Therren opened the door. “Ask him.”

***

Near dusk, they marched Tobious back to the ancestor terrace. The well's stone rim was bright with late sun. The statue of the woman with the bowl had been propped against the wall of the old storehouse, still wrapped in cloth, as though she were waiting for another household to claim her.

Samon was not among the workers clearing the approach. Tobious knew better than to hope he would be.

Darran and Therren stood beside a stranger in plain dark clothes. The man was older than the captain, his hair silver at the temples. He had the stillness of someone saving his breath for work, though when he turned, his left knee delayed him slightly. A narrow case hung at his side.

The stranger spoke to Darran in a language Tobious could not follow. Darran answered too quickly. The stranger held out his hand, and after a moment Darran gave him the drawing.

Then the man addressed Tobious in Aras speech, marked by an unfamiliar coast in the vowels.

“You touched the broken regulator.”

“I cleared mud from it.”

“And after that?”

“A man appeared. Then the passage opened. Something came through.”

The stranger did not ask whether Tobious had dreamed any of it. He unrolled the hide, traced a line near the pierced circle, and folded it away.

“I am Panjios.”

“Who sent you?”

“A warning from the coast. One of the older stations still sends a signal when this one wakes.”

“Older than what?”

“Older than the stones around it.”

Tobious glanced toward Darran. “He was trying to wake it.”

“Yes,” Panjios said. He did not make Darran bear the whole answer. “Someone supplied him with the drawing. I intend to learn who accepted the request and why.”

Darran gave a short laugh. “You brought the authorization.”

Panjios turned the hide over. Pressed into one corner was a small device of three lines enclosing a gap. Tobious recognized it: the same mark beside the order Darran had shown underground. On Panjios's case, a metal tag bore it in relief.

“The permission to inspect a buried site,” Panjios said, “is not permission to open an unstable route with unprotected workers.”

Therren cut in. “If the inspection is yours, inspect it. If it's unsafe, tell me how to keep my people alive. I have no men to spare for a dispute over whose seal means what.”

Panjios asked to see the lower chamber. He told the captain he would enter first. Tobious was brought along because Panjios needed him to describe exactly where the light had formed.

Below, the walls seemed ordinary. A broken basket lay where one worker had dropped it. Someone had washed the dead guard's blood from the lower step, and the dark water had carried it to the drain Tobious had opened.

Panjios stopped before the fallen beam. His fingers passed above it without touching. A dim line gathered around his forearm and widened until it surrounded him from shoulder to boot. It had no hard edge; Tobious saw it best where dust drifting down the stairs changed direction before reaching his clothes.

The soldier behind Tobious drew a breath.

Panjios looked back. “Only I am protected. Keep your distance.”

He approached the lower doorway. The black surface was inert. The bent clamp had fallen against the frame, trapping a band of green metal beneath it. Panjios knelt and opened the case at his side.

Inside lay a short dark blade with a narrow powered seam along one side. Its grip was wrapped in plain, unmarked material. It was not the great weapon Tobious had imagined when he had lain on the paving and wished for a sword. It looked like a tool someone had kept because it worked.

Panjios set the blade against the fouled metal.

The light around his body went out.

“Back,” he said.

He held the edge in place. A faint thread of brightness ran along the green band, then through it. Three breaths passed before the metal parted. Panjios withdrew the blade and a shimmer returned around him.

The soldier behind Tobious swore softly. Darran came down from the passage with another man and shoved past them to see the frame. The second man, a young guard with a bandage at his neck, saw the severed clamp and raised his spear toward Tobious.

“He did that yesterday,” the guard said. “My brother is dead.”

Panjios was still crouching. He turned between Tobious and the spear. Bronze struck the shimmer at his shoulder and stopped an arm's length from cloth. The impact pushed Panjios back against the stone. He did not look invulnerable. He let the force move him, found his footing, and lowered his empty hand.

“Your brother died,” he said. “No one here is going to make that untrue by killing another man.”

“He opened it.”

“The part I cut was already broken. The next opening was forced.”

The guard looked to Darran. Darran said nothing. Therren had followed them down; he took the spear from his soldier and sent him up with another man.

Tobious watched Panjios close the case. He had seen three things in less than a minute: a blow stopped in the air, a blade that could cut a substance unknown to any smith in Aras, and the brief interval when the one who held that blade had no protection at all.

“What was the thing?” Tobious asked.

“An Iwang.” Panjios glanced at the dark frame. “People on this shore have an older name for them. The Hollow Ones.”

“There are more?”

“Yes.”

Tobious wanted a gentler answer. He trusted this one further.

Panjios touched the place where the clamp had sat. “This station is shut for now. It won't stay that way without repair. The broken regulator used you to steady its first contact.”

“Used me how?”

“I can explain what we've measured. I won't pretend it answers everything.”

They returned to the light above. On the ancestor terrace, Lios was helping gather the fallen fragments of his jug into a cloth. He paused when Tobious emerged. A bruise darkened the side of his neck, but there was no rope on it.

Tobious stepped toward him. A soldier blocked the way.

“He isn't free,” Lios said before Tobious could ask.

The boy said it matter-of-factly, as one might report that the jug could not be mended. Then he bent back over the pieces.

***

Panjios asked to speak with Tobious where the captain could hear them. That was no assurance of honesty, but it changed the shape of the trap. Darran could no longer pretend that his instructions came from the same authority as Panjios's warning without being challenged.

“The damaged route needs a living reference,” Panjios said. “At present, that is you. If we try to force it from you, we may injure you and open it badly. If you learn to hold it safely, we may be able to repair what Darran damaged.”

“Where do you want to take me?”

“Across the coast station, to the city you saw.”

The city. The man who might be Tobious. The road falling with people on it. Tobious looked at the bay until the vessels moving there were just dark strokes against the sun.

“You can take me there,” he said, “and bring me back?”

“There are return procedures. They have limits. I won't promise a day I haven't measured.”

“What about Aras?”

“We can provision a small number of households and treat the injured while the route is secured.”

“All of them?”

“Not on the authority I carry.”

“Then ask for it.”

“I will. That is different from telling you it has been granted.”

Tobious looked at Therren. The captain's expression did not soften. He was weighing a future worker against an immediate one.

“Samon needs a splint made properly and something for pain,” Tobious said. “Lios goes to his own people. And the seed your men took gets replaced. Before I leave.”

“Those are three demands,” Therren said.

“You counted fourteen jars yesterday. You're able to count three.”

Panjios did not smile. “I can bring dressings and a brace fitted to the leg. I can bring grain equal to what I can carry through Salt Gate on the next scheduled opening. I can arrange for the boy's release from this labor assignment. Therren must agree to that last part.”

The captain glanced toward Lios. “A broken jug has made him expensive.”

“Then take my labor against it,” Tobious said.

“Your labor is already taken.”

Panjios said, “The stores I bring will be entered under my seal, not his. There will be a written count and a local witness.”

Therren thought for some time. At last he nodded once. “The boy goes to the village. Your supplies may pass. Darran will not touch the lower frame until your people repair it.”

Darran began to object. Therren silenced him with a hand.

Tobious had the sensation of watching men divide a field that belonged to neither of them. Yet Samon could receive care. Lios could sleep somewhere without a rope. A little grain could return. There was a price on each thing, and he would be the one to go.

“I haven't agreed,” he said.

Panjios met his eyes. “No.”

“I need to speak to Mariya.”

“Then speak to her. At the lower granary before morning. The supplies should be there by then.”

They released Tobious from the soldiers' hold. He reached Lios before anyone could call him back. The boy was tying the cloth around his broken jug.

“You can go home,” Tobious said.

Lios looked past him at Therren, waiting for the order to be taken back. The captain turned away.

“My aunt's on the lower street,” Lios said. “She'll be angry about the jug.”

“Let her be angry at me.”

“She knows who breaks things.”

Lios tucked the bundle under his arm and ran before a soldier could invent another task for him.

Tobious watched him go. The boy had asked to keep the pieces of a vessel he could no longer use. Perhaps his aunt would know how to mend it. Perhaps she would give him another and he would refuse, because this one was his.

Panjios came to stand beside Tobious, leaving him room to walk away.

“A city isn't an answer to a village,” Tobious said.

“No.”

“And a sword isn't one either.”

“It may help you bring an answer home.”

Tobious studied the older man's hand. Its knuckles bore small scars where the blade's case had rubbed them over years. It was a working hand, though it had learned a work Tobious did not know.

“Show Mariya the grain,” Tobious said. “Then we'll talk.”
