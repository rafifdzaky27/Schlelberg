# SCHLELBERG

## Chapter Four — Two Jars

Mariya found another false count before the sun had set.

The palace tablet named four Aras workers assigned to the lower storehouse. Mariya knew seven had been sent below: Samon, Tobious, and five others. Samon had returned on a litter. Tobious had returned under guard. The remaining five still needed finding, and the tablet left three of the seven without a name at all.

It might have been carelessness. The same man had written thirteen beside a levy of fourteen jars and had corrected the number only when she put her pebbles on the ground in front of him. He made mistakes most often where Aras stood to lose something.

She pressed the tablet flat between her palms so she would not break it. Breaking the record would give the captain a reason to charge her for its absence. She would need its marks the next time he claimed he had never taken the men.

At the lower granary, three households waited for food. Mariya had promised to open the door before dusk. Now dusk had come, and the two clay seals with the stamped bull barred her from opening the seed jars without being accused of theft.

She opened a side bin of lentils instead. Hessah's family received a measure less than they had last week. Thesia, whose husband had died at the gate, accepted a portion for herself and then asked for one for her sister's children. The sister's household was missing from the occupation list. There had been no adult name on their door when the scribe passed through.

“I can't put them under your name every time,” Mariya said.

“Then put them under their own.”

“I'm trying.”

Thesia looked at the measure of lentils in Mariya's hands. “Will they eat a name tonight?”

Mariya gave her the second portion. It had to come from a jar already counted for another household. She made a mark on the small waxed board she kept under her arm. By morning she would have to explain it to someone, perhaps to the household that had expected the missing measure.

She had made this kind of decision before the soldiers came. Once, after a poor olive pressing, she had followed the village allocation exactly. A widow who had moved into her daughter's empty house was left off the list and received nothing for three days. Mariya still remembered the clean, unbroken seal on that jar. There were ways of being honest with a record and false to the people it claimed to describe.

When the last household had gone, she sat on the granary step and counted what remained by touch. Pulse jars, two official seed jars, barley in smaller household stores she could not simply take. At the mill, they had flour enough to make tomorrow's bread if the soldiers kept to their own kitchen. If they did not, her numbers were wishes.

“You forgot to count yourself,” Nerissa said.

The midwife lowered herself beside Mariya with a jar of broth. Her back was bent from work, but her eyes missed little.

“I ate at noon.”

“A crust isn't a meal.”

“There was more.”

Nerissa opened the jar. The steam smelled of onion and a bone that had given all it could. “You can lie to a tablet. At least make the lie useful.”

Mariya took it. From Nerissa, this counted as an offer of peace.

“Samon?”

“I straightened the leg as much as I dared. The flesh has been badly crushed. He'll need it held still, and I cannot promise how much use he'll have after it heals.”

Mariya swallowed a mouthful of broth that was too hot. “Does he know?”

“He asked whether he'd walk to the spring again. I told him I would do what I could.”

“That's what people say when—”

“It's what I say when I know what I can do.” Nerissa eased herself up. “Eat the rest. Then come help me change the wrapping.”

Mariya watched the old woman go. In the yard below, the statue from the ancestor terrace stood against a wall under its cloth. Someone had left a cup of water at its feet. She hoped the soldiers would overlook it. She was tired of spending courage on whether a cup might be taken away.

***

The stranger's supplies arrived on two handcarts after nightfall.

Mariya met them with Nerissa and a lamp. Panjios himself pushed one cart. A younger porter pushed the other, accompanied by a Telassari soldier who looked unhappy to be there. Therren's scribe carried a fresh tablet. Darran was absent.

“Show me what you brought,” Mariya said.

Panjios began with the grain. He lifted the lids of two sealed containers and allowed her to weigh their contents on the granary balance. Barley, clean and dry, nearly enough to fill two of the village's jars. Not the twelve that had left yesterday. It would feed people while the remaining seed was protected; it would not make the occupation harmless.

She took a handful and let it run through her fingers. The kernels were whole. In the lamp's light she could see no powder from insects.

“Where did you get this?”

“From the coastal stores assigned to our service route.”

“Can it be sown?”

“The supply record says yes.”

“I can read the king's tablet too. That doesn't make it true.”

Panjios held out his hand. She gave him the kernels. He put them back in the container without offense.

“Keep some aside to test. I won't ask you to stake a field on my record.”

The scribe began recording the grain under Therren's seal. Mariya placed one of her pebbles on his tablet.

“Measure it with ours,” she said.

The soldier shifted his weight. Panjios looked at the scribe. For a moment Mariya thought he might invoke whatever authority had brought the grain. Instead he asked her for the wooden measure she trusted. She fetched it. Two complete jar loads and a smaller remainder were counted in both men’s presence, then carried inside under her direction.

The medicine came next. Clean wrappings, a brace made from a substance lighter than wood, a vial whose contents Panjios named but did not ask them to use blindly. Nerissa took the brace to Samon. She insisted on seeing the adjustment done while she watched, so that if it shifted in the night she would know how to set it back.

Samon lay in his sister's back room, sweating despite the cool air. He cursed when the old wrapping came free, then apologized to no one in particular.

“Don't waste your voice,” Nerissa told him.

“I thought you'd ask how it feels.”

“I can see how it feels.”

Panjios knelt at the end of the mat, showed her where the brace opened, and asked permission before lifting Samon's leg. Mariya noticed that he waited for Samon to answer. Darran had treated bodies underground as parts of a machine; the distinction mattered even if it could be practiced by someone serving the same distant seal.

The brace held the leg without pressing on the worst swelling. Samon's jaw loosened when the weight was taken off it. Nerissa inspected each point of contact and adjusted one strap herself.

“It works for tonight,” she said. “Tomorrow is another count.”

Panjios accepted that verdict.

Outside, Mariya asked him how many people his coast station could carry away.

“Under present authorization? Exceptional patients. A small team. Supplies.”

“That's not what I asked.”

“The opening can take people. The crossing isn't the part that's forbidden.”

“So whose rule is it?”

Panjios glanced at the occupied ridge across the valley. “Several people have made that rule useful to themselves.”

“Including your people?”

“Yes.”

His answer was brief. Mariya preferred it to a speech, but it did not buy him her trust. If the rule could be changed, then every day it remained was somebody's decision. She thought of Thesia's sister and the missing names on the clay.

“Can Tobious come home after he goes?”

“There is a return procedure. I won't promise that a dangerous site will become safe on a date I choose.”

“You'll have to say that to him.”

“I have.”

“Say it again.”

***

Tobious arrived before dawn, escorted as far as the granary gate and then allowed to walk the last stretch himself. His lip was swollen. He had bound his right wrist in a strip of old linen, badly; the knot pressed where the swelling was greatest.

Mariya untied it without asking. He watched her hands.

“You always pull the knot toward the sore place,” she said.

“It stays tied.”

“So does a trap.”

She rewound the cloth. It was easier to look at his wrist than at his face. Yesterday she had sent him below because he knew the channels. That had been reasonable; the reason had not saved Samon's leg or the dead guard. She had given his name to the officer without asking him, and he knew it. The words she needed were both defense and apology. She could not make either stand in for the other.

“I should have told you first,” she said.

“I wouldn't have gone.”

“I know.”

“Then you still would have told them.”

Mariya finished the knot. “I might have. We needed someone below who knew the drain. I should have let you answer for yourself.”

He flexed his fingers once and winced. “They found something they wanted. I don't think Darran knew what would answer when he opened it.”

“Did you?”

“No.”

He glanced at the two protected seed jars behind her. The clay seals remained unbroken. It was a small sight, but she saw him take steadiness from it.

“Panjios brought grain,” she said. “Two jars' worth, and a little more. The kernels look sound. Nerissa says the brace helped Samon.”

“Lio?”

“At his aunt's. He made her keep the broken jug. She says she'll make a cup from the bottom.”

Tobious exhaled. It was the first unguarded breath she had seen him take since he arrived.

“He asked me to go,” he said.

“I heard.”

“I wanted to say no.”

“What changed?”

“I saw that place.” He looked over the granary wall toward the sea. “He was there. A man with my face. I don't know if he was waiting for me or if it was already happening somewhere I can't reach.”

Mariya let the silence stay long enough for him to hear himself. Then she asked, “What can Panjios teach you there?”

“How to keep the door from opening. Maybe how to go through it without bringing another of those things home.”

“That's useful. It's a reason to go.”

“You don't want me to?”

“I want you here. I also want this place to have more than two seed jars and a guard who knows he can take the rest.”

“I could bring everyone across.”

“Panjios says the crossing allows it. Their rules don't.”

“Rules change.”

“Sometimes. Learn which ones are walls and which ones are men standing in front of a doorway.”

He gave her a sidelong look. “You sound as if you've already made me leave.”

“I haven't made you do anything.”

That, at least, was true. She knew how easy it was to turn love into a claim over someone else's choice. She had come close yesterday without giving it that name.

They went to the granary's rear steps. In the narrow space beneath the fig tree, the morning was still cool. Tobious sat with his injured hand resting on his knee. Mariya leaned against the wall beside him, their shoulders touching.

“When this is over,” he said, “I want a week when the only thing we count is how many figs you take before they dry.”

“You'd have to stop taking them first.”

“I could learn.”

“That's a more difficult training than Panjios is offering.”

He laughed once, surprised by it. Then his face changed. “I mean it.”

“I know.”

She looked down at their hands. His left lay open on the step between them. She put hers beside it, close enough that the sides of their fingers met. There was no promise in the contact that could make a road safe or a village free. She wanted it anyway.

“I want the week too,” she said.

For a while they listened to people opening shutters along the lane.

“I'll stay here,” she said. “I'll find out who they send below and where. You come back with what you learn. Don't come back with a promise that everyone will be safe.”

He turned his hand beneath hers. “Then what do I promise?”

“That you'll tell me what you actually know.”

He agreed. In the first light, they worked out something less beautiful and more useful than a vow. Mariya would scratch the leaf from his family's jar onto any supply wrapper she sent through Panjios's next delivery, with a second mark beneath it for the number of workers she had counted. Tobious would ask at the coast station when a return message could travel. If no message came, neither would pretend the silence meant consent.

Below them, the mill began to turn. Somebody had found enough grain to keep it working for another day.
