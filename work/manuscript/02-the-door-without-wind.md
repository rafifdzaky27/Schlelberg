# SCHLELBERG

## Chapter Two — The Door Without Wind

The man with Tobious's face vanished while Tobious was still trying to reach him.

He pulled free of the overseer and stepped into the water. The black surface held a pale street, a broken road, the place where the man had stood. Tobious could see between the towers now. Whatever had fallen from the road was lost beneath them.

“Move back,” the soldier on the stairs said.

Tobious heard him without understanding why the words were meant for him. He knew the line beneath the stranger's eye. He knew the voice. He had seen himself bleeding in a place beyond the span of any road he had ever imagined.

He reached for the light.

The overseer caught him by the collar. “Tell me what he said.”

“You heard him.”

“Tell me what you did.”

In the passage behind the soldier, clay broke against stone. The water boy had dropped his jug. Tobious saw him appear at the bend, the rope still around his neck, one hand pressed to his mouth. The soldier turned long enough to shout at him to get out.

Tobious used the moment to take the wooden rod from the water. He pushed it toward the opening. It struck something hard where the distant street appeared to begin.

The impact jarred his swollen wrist. The rod slipped from his hand and fell into the chamber. He watched its ripples spread through the shallow water. None crossed the black line.

The man had not been standing in a doorway. Tobious had seen him as one sees a figure at the bottom of a still well: near enough to recognize, impossibly far to touch.

The towers thinned. Light passed through the man's empty place. Then the surface went black again.

For a breath, nobody moved.

“A picture,” the soldier said.

The overseer shook his head. He had let go of Tobious and was already drawing the rolled hide from his tube. He held it close to the lamp, glancing from the marks to the green clamp in the frame.

“There should be a second contact.”

“Should be?” Tobious asked.

The overseer lowered the drawing. There was a small circle pierced through the hide at its edge, and beside it a mark Tobious had seen on the soldier's requisition tablet that morning. Not a Telassari seal. Something stamped for men who could ask a king's soldiers to dig beneath graves.

The guard came down two steps. “Darran. We should fetch the captain.”

So the overseer had a name. Darran examined the broken end of the clamp, then wedged the butt of the soldier's spare spear beneath it.

“Hold it there.”

“The captain—”

“Will ask why you stood and watched it close.”

The soldier braced the spear. Tobious backed toward the stairs. The boy was still at the bend, staring at the puddle of his lost water. He had a narrow face and a freshly rubbed patch of skin where the neck rope lay.

“Go up,” Tobious told him.

The boy hesitated. He looked at the soldier.

Tobious understood. The boy had been ordered to bring water below. He did not yet know whether fear of this place was a reason anyone would accept.

Darran pushed the damaged clamp into its recess. Metal clicked against metal. Tobious felt pressure in his teeth, as if he were descending too quickly into deep water. The black surface bent inward. The lamp flames leaned toward it again.

This time the water moved.

A breeze touched his wet ankles and brought a smell he could not place: hot stone after rain, spoiled salt, something sweet beneath it. On the other side stood no shattered road. He saw a narrow place lined with pale walls, lit without a flame. The air at the threshold drew at his tunic.

“That's open,” he said.

Darran's delight was so immediate it frightened him more than the breach.

“Keep it steady.”

The soldier shifted his grip on the spear. In the bright passage beyond, a shape moved against the wall. Tobious thought it was a man approaching sideways to fit through a confined gap. Then the shape unfolded a second set of limbs.

The boy made a sound. Tobious turned and saw that the soldier had barred the stair with the length of his spear to keep him down. The order must have been to collect the broken jug. The boy crouched on the lowest step, too frightened to pass either way.

“Let him go,” Tobious said.

The soldier did not look at him. His eyes were on the pale passage.

Something placed a hand against the threshold. It had too many joints and no nails. The hand went thin as smoke, then gathered itself again when the air flowed over it. A long shoulder followed. Where a chest ought to have been, Tobious saw the bright wall beyond, distorted as though viewed through flowing water.

The creature turned its head toward the boy.

The soldier released the clamp. The spear butt struck the chamber floor. He snatched his own weapon from against the stair and thrust. Bronze passed through the creature's hollow middle. The point rang against the far wall.

The creature closed around the shaft.

Its outline hardened for the space of a heartbeat. At its collar, where the neck narrowed into the empty torso, a dark knot appeared, wet and ridged. The soldier pulled back. Bronze scraped it. The creature convulsed, and the smell of burned salt filled the chamber.

It struck him with one of its limbs. His hide shield took the blow and folded against him. The force drove him against the stair. He screamed for men above.

Tobious had already reached the boy.

He seized the rope at the child's neck and dragged him toward the passage. The rope caught under the soldier's foot. The boy fell. Tobious pulled again, felt the child's cry through the line, and stopped. With his left hand he found the knot. It had swollen wet and tight.

“Your name,” he said, though he had no idea why he asked now.

The boy stared at him.

“What do they call you?”

“Lios.”

“Lio. Stay against the wall.”

He put the rope around a stone projection and hauled until it slipped free of the soldier's boot. His right hand failed him halfway through. He wound it once around his left forearm and drew Lios past the fallen man.

The soldier thrust again. This time the spear point found the collar knot. A spray of dark grains struck the steps. The creature pulled itself away from the bronze, and its lower limbs blurred. The guard tried to stand. Its hand closed around the back of his neck.

Tobious did not see the moment he died. He heard the spear fall.

Darran was at the clamp, trying to wrench it free with both hands. His lamp lay in the water, one wick still burning. As he pulled, the open passage widened another finger's breadth. The creature turned toward him. He abandoned the metal and stumbled past Tobious up the steps.

At the circular chamber, Samon and two other workers were running back with their baskets and ropes. They had heard the screaming. The sound of boots followed them from the upper stairs.

“Out,” Darran ordered. “Seal it.”

“How?” Samon said.

The creature entered the lower passage. Its limbs touched both walls. Each time it reached into the cooler air away from the light, it loosened at the edges, then drew substance again from the open threshold behind it. Tobious saw the rhythm. It was not following them on a body it could keep for long. The doorway was feeding it.

He looked up at the rope Samon had tied around the lifted slab. One end ran through a notch in the masonry to a wooden lever on the chamber floor. He had used it an hour earlier to raise a basket of mud. The beam could shift the slab a little. It could not lift the whole doorway.

But the clamp sat beneath its near corner.

“Samon. The rope.”

“Leave it.”

“The metal is keeping the door open.”

Lios pressed himself behind one of the basket carriers. Samon looked from him to the lower passage. His face changed. He had chosen to believe Tobious.

“You two, take the boy upstairs,” he told the workers. “Tell them the stair is blocked.”

One woman picked up Lios and carried him despite his protests that he could walk. The second worker ran beside her. Darran went after them, shouting for soldiers.

The creature came into the chamber.

It paused beside the lamp. Light passed through its open middle and made moving bars across the old stones. Tobious could see no eyes. Still, he felt it attend to the two men left there, and to the rope Samon was fastening around the lever.

“Where does the drain lead?” Samon asked.

“Down the west channel.”

“Then if the roof comes?”

“The water goes that way.”

Samon gave him a flat look. “I meant us.”

They hauled. The rope stretched; the slab shifted no more than the width of Tobious's thumbnail. The creature moved. Samon let go to throw his basket at it. Wicker struck the wall. The Iwang flowed around it, thinning through the weave, then became heavy again as its collar came near the old stone.

Tobious took the lever with his left hand. The right could only steady it. He set his foot against the base and pulled until the wood bent. The slab moved another fraction. A line of green metal appeared beneath it.

“A stone,” he said. “Put it under.”

Samon jammed a loose block into the gap. The Iwang struck at his back. He rolled away; one limb touched his tunic and left a hole through wool and skin. He made no sound. He only caught the rope again, grimacing.

Together they hauled. Tobious thought of the sluice gate above his own field, which never yielded to a straight pull after silt dried in the channel. Lift, wedge, release, lift again. The work did not care whether he was afraid.

The creature gathered itself. At the lower stair the guard's bronze spear lay half in the water. Tobious let go of the beam, caught it with his left hand, and swept the point across the Iwang's collar. He was not quick enough to stab. Bronze touched the knot for an instant. The creature recoiled from the contact.

“Now,” Tobious said.

Samon pulled the rope alone. Tobious shoved the stone wedge into the new space. The slab dropped against it. The clamp snapped sideways out of its recess with a sound like a jar cracking at the kiln.

Air rushed down the passage. Then it stopped.

The light in the lower chamber began to narrow. The Iwang recoiled through the doorway, drawing itself along the wall with its limbs. It did not flee from Tobious or his poor strike. It followed the vanishing current. For a moment its collar knot hung in the passage, solid enough to cast a shadow. Then it passed beneath the slab and into the shrinking seam.

The floor lurched.

Samon threw himself at Tobious and drove him clear of the lever. Stone fell between them. A beam from the old ceiling hit Samon's left leg and pinned him below the knee.

Tobious landed against the wall. Dust filled his mouth. The chamber had gone dark except for the dropped lamp and a thin, declining line of light under the lower stone. It narrowed to nothing.

“Samon?”

“Here.”

Tobious crawled to him. The older man's leg lay twisted beneath the beam. He touched it once and took his hand away.

“Don't pull me,” Samon said.

The command came through clenched teeth. Tobious wanted to lift the stone. He could not even make his right hand close around its edge.

People shouted above. He called to them for poles, for wedges, for someone who knew how to set a crushed limb. He could name what was needed. That was the only strength he had left.

Darran returned with soldiers. They raised the beam a little at a time while Samon bit through a fold of his tunic. At the upper passage, Tobious saw Lios sitting against a wall, alive, his broken jug arranged carefully between his knees. Someone had cut the rope from his neck.

The boy lifted the two pieces of the jug when he saw Tobious. “It was full,” he said.

“I know.”

Lios looked back down the stair toward the dead guard. Tobious wished he had a better thing to say. He had saved the boy, and the boy would still have to remember the sound of that spear falling.

At the top of the stairs, the sky had begun to dim. Aras remained where it had been all day: the roofs, the bay, a line of women coming from the well with vessels balanced against their hips. The world had made room for a door, then closed around it without so much as changing the color of the evening.

Darran walked beside the men carrying Samon. He stopped when they reached the yard.

“The boy goes to his household,” he told the soldiers. “The injured man gets a litter. This one stays.”

He pointed at Tobious.

“Samon needs someone who can tell them how the beam fell.”

“They can ask you tomorrow.”

Tobious glanced at the empty place beside the well where the statue with the bowl had stood. He thought of the man in the impossible city, of blood at his right ribs, of a warning spoken too late to tell him what to do now.

Two soldiers took him by the arms. His swollen wrist flared when one tightened his grip. He went with them because Samon was breathing on the litter, Lios had been carried into the lane, and the black surface beneath their feet had gone quiet.

He had closed a door. He had no idea how long it would stay closed.
