# SCHLELBERG

## Chapter Five — The Far Shore

Tobious spent the morning repairing a door he did not want anyone to use.

The lower frame remained sealed, but water had begun to rise behind the stones dropped in the collapse. If it filled the chamber, pressure would find a crack. Tobious showed Panjios where the old west drain met the bay channel and watched the older man order a new brace placed above the broken clamp. Darran stood on the far side of the chamber with Therren's men, permitted to observe and forbidden to touch the frame.

Tobious had drawn the short straw for fastening the drain grate. His right wrist was bound, so he worked with his left hand while a village mason held the bar. Every task took longer than it ought to. Twice he dropped the peg. Once the mason picked it up without remark. The second time she said, “You can hold the lamp.”

He gave her the tool. She had come below because her father knew the old walls and she knew how he had repaired them. Tobious knew the water. Between them, the chamber stayed dry. He wondered whether the future city had people who spent their days refusing to let ancient stone fail.

At the top of the stairs, Samon waited on a chair brought from his sister's house. He had insisted on coming to see the brace before Tobious left. The fitted support held his leg straight, and someone had given him a stick he could not yet use.

“The east joint?” Samon asked.

“Packed. The overflow goes west.”

“Until the next rain.”

“The mason knows.”

Samon watched the woman follow Panjios out of the passage. “She does.”

Tobious had expected approval. Instead Samon looked toward the channel leading under the village.

“Who's taking the sluice above your field?”

“Mariya has it arranged.”

“With whom?”

“The millers. And the mason's brother.”

“She didn't ask me.”

“You told her yesterday you would break the other leg before you let a novice adjust it.”

Samon grunted. “Then she listened.”

Tobious crouched so they were eye to eye. Samon was a man he had known since he was small enough to carry one end of a reed mat while Samon bore the other. There were things Tobious wished to say about the beam and the rope, but Samon had not asked for thanks or apology. He had asked about the sluice.

“I'll come back,” Tobious said.

Samon looked at the right hand Tobious kept close to his body. “Come back with that healed. I don't need two useless repairers.”

It was as much blessing as Samon would give in front of Darran.

***

Mariya walked with Tobious as far as the path descending from the lower terraces. She carried a small bundle of bread, figs, and a change of linen. He told her Panjios had supplies enough to feed him. She handed him the bundle anyway.

“This is food from here,” she said. “You'll know what you've eaten.”

The morning wind moved the olive leaves in pale waves. Below the fields, the bay lay blue and ordinary. Fishermen were drawing in their boats under the watch of two soldiers on the beach. Tobious had walked this path all his life, carrying baskets to the shore. Today every bend seemed to belong to some earlier version of himself.

They stopped where the last field wall gave way to scrub.

“Panjios says the clocks move together,” Tobious said. “When I spend a day there, you spend one here.”

“He showed me the return register.”

“You checked?”

“It had dates, names, and arrivals written in two hands. It could still be false. It was better than a promise with no names in it.”

“You shouldn't have to count every word anyone says to us.”

“Then they should stop giving us reasons.”

He knew the line of her mouth when she was tired. He could have reached for the old easy argument, told her that when he returned, she could rest. She would have called it a lovely plan and then shown him the list of things that needed doing before sunset.

Instead he asked, “What will you do today?”

“Ask why the lower labor tablet lost three names. Send Thesia's sister a portion under her own household. Talk to the boatmen while they're angry enough to answer.”

“You planned all that before breakfast?”

“I planned the last one during breakfast.”

She adjusted the bundle on his shoulder. The cloth brushed his neck. He wanted her hand to stay there, and felt ashamed that he wanted it when she had so much left to do.

“You can want me here,” she said.

He looked at her.

“You keep treating it like an accusation.”

“I don't want to leave you to this.”

“I know. I don't want to be left. Both things can be true.”

The village bell sounded from above, a bronze dish struck when water was drawn for the common store. He had never thought he would miss that badly made noise.

“If I find a way to bring you through—”

“Tell me what it costs before you decide for me.”

He nodded. She had given him the same condition yesterday. It had sounded simple then. Now he understood that the distance he was about to cross could make truth harder than reassurance.

He took her hand with his left. She leaned forward and rested her forehead against his for a breath. The touch was brief, but it outlasted the bell.

“Come back honest, Tobi,” she said.

Panjios waited where the path turned toward the shore. He watched the sea and allowed them their time. When Tobious joined him, Mariya was already walking uphill. She did not turn. Tobious understood that she had work to do; he watched until the wall hid her from sight.

***

It took them forty minutes to reach the salt flats and another twenty to follow the broken shore to the abandoned storage caves. Tobious counted the turns. If he had to return alone, he wanted to know where the sea cut off the path at high water and where a person could climb the rocks.

The cave mouth had been disguised with a wall of collapsed jars and brushwood. Panjios entered first. Inside, the air was dry enough to sting Tobious's throat. Salt had left white fur in the cracks of the stone. Men had once scraped it into baskets here; their grooves remained in the floor.

Three people waited beside a low frame of dark material fitted into the back wall. One checked Panjios's tag against a tablet that gave off its own soft light. Another inspected the bundle at Tobious's shoulder. The third stood by a shallow basin whose surface remained smooth despite the draft from the cave mouth.

“The village station was damaged,” Panjios told Tobious. “This one is scheduled, watched, and used for small loads. You may still turn back.”

Tobious looked at the basin. A lantern's flame leaned toward the frame as a line of brightness formed along it. The water stirred; a cold, clean current came through the opening. Whatever appeared this time, air was moving. There would be a floor to step onto.

“If I cross, how do I know when I can return?”

The clerk turned the tablet so Panjios could read it. He translated the entry for Tobious, then made the clerk repeat the terms slowly. The register named a receiving station, an outgoing opening time, and a corresponding day on this shore. Some dates were marked delayed, with a reason beside each; several had been completed. Tobious could not read the marks, but Panjios pointed to the people standing there who had traveled through on previous schedules. They described the same number of days elapsed on both sides.

“And if the route is closed?” Tobious asked.

“You wait for authorization or a safe diagnostic opening,” Panjios said. “I will not pretend that the return is yours to command today.”

It was a hard answer to give to a man standing by a door. Tobious believed Panjios wanted him to cross. He also believed the man had left room for refusal. That room mattered.

Tobious put his palm on the dry stone beside the frame. He thought of the old chamber, the guard's spear falling, Samon's leg under a beam. Then of Mariya's fingers on his hand. The two thoughts did not balance like grain on a scale. One did not cancel the other.

“I want the next message window written for me,” he said. “And for Mariya.”

Panjios spoke to the clerk. A mark was entered beside Tobious's name, with Mariya's scratched leaf beside it.

Tobious stepped through.

The floor did not disappear. He felt one change of pressure, like entering deep shade after walking in sun. The cave fell behind him. Ahead was a room of light and smooth walls, crowded with cases, workers, and a smell of warm metal. A woman in gray asked him to move clear of the receiving line. Panjios repeated the instruction in his language, but Tobious was already stepping aside. People behind him needed to come through.

He turned once. Through the gate he saw the salt cave, its white walls and broken jars. The clerk on that side raised a hand in farewell to the people beside him. A field narrowed across the view; the cave was gone.

He had crossed centuries, though the movement itself had taken less time than lifting a jar.

***

The city called Veyr climbed the cliffs where Tobious had known only a ridge and a citadel.

Panjios led him onto an open walk. Above them, roads crossed through empty air. Some were broad enough for whole crowds; others carried narrow silver carriages without horses, their motion so smooth that Tobious had to watch their shadows to see how fast they went. Along one wall, water rose in clear tubes to terraces planted with unfamiliar green. Higher still, a surface darkened as the sun struck it, shading windows beneath. The city seemed to know the heat before its inhabitants did.

At the far end of the valley stood towers layered with gardens. Their upper reaches caught cloud shadow; their lower floors opened onto workshops, food stalls, and passages under repair. People wore fabrics that moved easily with them, some patched and some so fine that no seam showed. A child rode in a wheeled chair that followed her father's hand. A worker carried a basket of fruit on his head in the old way. Tobious had expected the future to make the past impossible to recognize. It had found room for both kinds of effort.

He stopped at the edge of the walk. Through the open space below, he could see the bay. Its shoreline had been moved. A long wall now reached into the water where his village's small boats would have beached. For a moment he could not place Aras at all. Then he found the shape of the hill and knew that somewhere beneath these roads lay the stone his mother had once touched.

“How many people live here?” he asked.

“More than I could count for you without a register,” Panjios said.

“That many?”

“I think you'd object if I guessed.”

Tobious smiled despite himself.

They boarded a carriage that ran inside a clear-sided channel. Tobious kept one hand around a rail. At the first turn, a voice spoke from the wall. He understood Panjios's reply but none of the written signs that changed above the doors. He tried to keep the symbols in mind. They changed faster than he could name their shapes.

The carriage stopped partway through the climb.

At first nobody complained. A woman closed her eyes and leaned against the wall. Then the lights dimmed and a low alarm sounded twice. Panjios asked the driver a question. She answered in a rush; Tobious caught only the translated words *carrier delay* and *service tier*.

Below the channel, people in gray work coats emerged onto a narrow maintenance walk. One held up a darkened unit from the carriage's side. Another passed her a replacement, but the bracket would not seat. A queue gathered behind the stopped carriage, and heat began to collect inside it.

“Can we open this?” Tobious asked.

“Not while the next carrier might move,” Panjios said. He touched a panel beside the door and spoke to the driver. A vent opened above them, letting cooler air in.

Through the floor Tobious could see the repair team working. The towers and bridges had seemed to hang by will alone. Here were bolts, hands, a woman asking for a different tool because the first had slipped. The city was held up by people who knew where it failed.

When the carriage moved again, the passengers continued their interrupted talk. For Tobious the delay stayed with him. Wonder had acquired a sound: the knock of a worker fitting a stubborn bracket into place.

At the upper stop, Panjios guided him away from a gallery whose walls displayed bright images of armed figures. Tobious slowed at the sight of blades.

“Later,” Panjios said. “First, someone looks at your wrist.”

“It works.”

“You held the rail with the other hand for the entire ride.”

Tobious looked down. The binding Mariya had tied remained firm. He had wanted the city to see him as the man who closed a dangerous door. Panjios had seen a patient protecting a swollen joint.

They entered a clinic beneath a roof that filtered the afternoon light into a gentle gold. A man with a burn on his arm waited beside a woman asleep against a stack of folded coats. Nobody asked which of them had come from an ancient shore before bringing them water.

Tobious took the cup offered to him. It was clear and light and did not break when he set it down too hard. He thought of Lios collecting the pieces of his clay jug. An entire city lay between the two boys' cups, and the distance had been crossed in a step.

He drank. Then he gave the cup back and followed Panjios toward the examination room.
