# SCHLELBERG

## Chapter Eight — The Weight of a Shield

On the sixth morning after the levy, while his wrist still held the shape of the soldier's blow, Tobious learned that a shield could not save him from his own feet.

Suss gave him a wooden training blade and told him to reach a painted line on the floor. A smaller trainee stood between him and it, protected by a field that showed only when the ceiling light slid across it. Tobious advanced as he would have gone at a man beside the granary door: fast, square-shouldered, determined not to be pushed back.

The trainee stepped half a pace aside. Tobious's wooden blade struck the field and skidded. She touched his unguarded ribs with hers. He tried to turn. His front foot caught behind the rear, and he fell.

The floor was softer than stone. His bruised chest did not care.

“Why did she reach you?” Suss asked.

“I missed.”

“You gave her your whole weight before you knew where she would be.”

Tobious sat up. “The shield stopped the blade.”

“It redirected one short blow. Then she moved. The shield didn't move your feet.”

Suss offered him a hand. Tobious took it with his left. The commander did not mention the right wrist. Edda had written its restrictions on his training card, and Suss had read them.

They tried again. This time Tobious watched the trainee's shoulder instead of the line. He kept his stance. She took a step toward him, he hesitated, and her blade touched his forearm before he could retreat.

“Better feet,” Suss said. “Bad distance.”

By the ninth attempt Tobious could feel the difference between advancing and falling forward. He still could not reach the painted line. The trainee had trained for years. Her victory did not surprise anyone but him.

At the rest bell, he set down the wooden blade and found a cup of water. The room had clean walls, even light, a floor that softened when someone fell. At the granary, seven soldiers and a hard stone threshold had taught him the same lesson once. Here they had made a place where he could survive learning it again and again. He resented the difference as much as he was grateful for it.

Suss brought him a small shield unit, no larger than a fist, fitted to a band worn at the ribs.

“This protects against sudden force,” he said. “It won't hold up a roof. It won't stop a slow hand from moving you. If you couple a powered blade to another field, your own full protection drops until the contact resolves.”

He demonstrated with one of the experienced operators. Their blades met close enough that Tobious could hear a faint pulse between them. Suss held his edge there while the operator counted three heartbeats. His shield went pale, then disappeared at the moment his blade passed through a small marked sector of her field. A second operator stepped into his exposed side, raising cover while he drew back.

“Why bring the second person?” Tobious asked.

“Because I would have been unprotected where you stood.”

The first operator, Jorra, took off her headpiece and wiped sweat from her brow. The other, Sennos, reset the timing board. They had the movements of people who trusted one another enough to complain about each other's habits. Sennos accused Jorra of counting too slowly. Jorra told him the shield did not care how flattering his numbers were.

Tobious looked at the narrow place where Suss's protection had failed. In Aras he had wanted a sword because the soldiers had one and he did not. Here a sword demanded that someone else keep him alive while he used it.

“Why a blade?” he asked. “You have machines that can move a carriage over empty air. Can none of them throw a point faster than a man?”

Sennos went to a cabinet and brought out a practice launcher. Its short bolt had a blunt head and a cord fastened to the shaft. He checked the cord's length with Jorra, then fired at her field from the marked distance. The bolt struck the faint light around her, lost its forward force across a wide curve, and fell. Tobious heard the head hit the floor before he heard the launcher settle in Sennos's hands.

“It still hurt,” Jorra said. “Don't believe anyone who tells you the field eats a blow. It spreads one. A heavier bolt could move me; several might overheat this unit.”

Sennos reeled the bolt in. On the second pass he aimed at a shield stand with no one behind it. The stand's warning rose after three impacts. Suss stopped the demonstration before the fourth.

“Why stop?” Tobious asked.

“Because now you've seen what happens when a defender has time to turn,” Suss said. “The next bolt would tell you only how much this stand costs to repair.”

He took the training blade and walked slowly toward Jorra. She let him bring its wooden edge close to her field. When he held it there, the shield did not throw him away as it had thrown the bolt; its light gathered at the point of contact. He made no cut. The exercise had no powered edge to complete one.

“Something held against one place can find a weakness,” he said. “Our blades use that time. The person holding one must come close enough to be struck back. That is why the other shield matters.”

Tobious thought of the spear at his neck in the granary yard. A Telassari archer could loose an arrow from beyond the threshing floor. Against these fields it would usually fail; against the workers behind them it would not. He asked whether Veyr had weapons large enough to break a shield from afar.

“Of course,” Suss said. “Not ones I will turn on a cooling station with people inside, or on a root chamber whose walls are holding two cities apart.” He returned the wooden blade to Tobious. “There are places where destroying the room wins a war. Our work begins when the room must survive.”

Tobious took the blade. He understood why a Warden learned to stand close. He did not yet know whether a commander's promise to preserve a room included everyone standing in it.

***

The first days were short. Edda examined the wrist after every session and sent him away when the swelling returned. Suss accepted her limits without praising Tobious for obeying them. Panjios gave him tasks that required looking and thinking while his hand recovered.

On the tenth day they brought him to a training channel beneath the Civic Spine. Water ran through three clear-walled courses above a maintenance floor. If the downstream gate opened, a marker boat would pass safely. If they chose the wrong branch, it would strike a barrier. The exercise was meant to teach reading a system before cutting into it.

“You know water,” Panjios said. “Tell me where the boat will go.”

Tobious watched the small eddies gather at the west fork. “That one. The eastern channel looks open, but the water leans away from it.”

He was right. The marker passed west. For an instant he felt the ease of being useful at something he already knew.

“Now change it,” Panjios said.

Tobious found a gate wheel and turned it. Nothing moved. He put his shoulder against the rim, then stopped when a sound came from inside the wall: a low, rising strain. Panjios did not intervene. Ypin, who had been sent to keep the exercise from damaging a real pump, appeared from the service hatch.

“That's a powered valve,” he said. “The wheel is an emergency release. Keep forcing it and you'll snap the hold.”

“Then why put a wheel on it?”

“For when the power fails.”

“It hasn't.”

“No.”

Tobious let go. He had treated a machine from this century as though it were the stubborn sluice above his field. Both moved water; they did not yield to the same hand.

Ypin opened a panel and showed him the valve's state. One line indicated commanded position. Another showed where the gate actually sat. They did not match because silt had lodged against the lower lip.

“Which of those is true?” Tobious asked.

“Both. One says what someone asked. One says what the water got.”

Tobious looked at the marker boat waiting upstream. “Then we clear the lip.”

They shut the feed, drained a small section, and worked side by side with short scrapers. Tobious's wrist tired quickly. Ypin handed him a lighter tool without comment. When the valve moved cleanly, the water changed direction and the marker slipped down the east course.

Panjios wrote something on his card. Tobious could not read it, so he made Panjios say it aloud.

“You recognized the flow. You stopped when your first method failed. You asked for a different skill.”

“And if I had kept turning?”

“You'd be helping Ypin repair the whole assembly tonight.”

“He already is,” Ypin said. “I have to check what your shoulder did to it.”

Panjios left them with the panel open. Ypin inspected the housing while Tobious held a light. The technician showed him which parts were accessible to a maintenance worker and which still required an authorization from the root staff. Tobious asked why a man responsible for pumps could not open every panel. Ypin said that keeping someone from making a mistake was one reason. Keeping a fault from being noticed was sometimes another.

“Which reason is this?” Tobious asked.

“I haven't decided.”

It was the first time Ypin had said *I don't know* in a way that invited Tobious to look with him.

On the following day Suss gave Tobious a shield exercise with no blade. Two trainees stood at opposite ends of a short corridor. A barrier would send a harmless but forceful pulse toward them, first from one side and then from the other. Tobious could extend his field across one opening at a time. He placed himself halfway between the trainees and waited for the first warning.

The pulse came from the side he expected. He covered the nearer trainee, then ran to the far end while his shield unit warned of heat. By the time he arrived, the second pulse had struck the other trainee's practice field. The exercise ended without anyone hurt. Suss asked what Tobious had done well.

“I reached both.”

“You reached the second after her own shield took the hit. What would you have done if she had been a patient with no field?”

Tobious looked at the corridor. He had treated his body as the only movable shield. Jorra showed him a stand that could hold the first opening while he covered the second. It required setting the stand before the warning, accepting that he could not know which side would be struck first. He resisted because choosing one place felt like giving up on the other. In the next run he placed the stand and called the two trainees by name before the pulse. Both had cover. He reached neither with a dramatic sprint.

“Better,” Suss said. “A shield protects where it is. You cannot make it protect where you wish you had been.”

Afterward Tobious sat with his wrist in Edda's cool support and watched the others reset the corridor. He could not train away the time he had been absent from Aras. He could learn to leave a place safe for someone else to stand. It was a less satisfying form of strength than he had imagined at the granary. It was beginning to look more useful.

***

In the days that followed, the clinic released Tobious's wrist from its support for longer periods. Edda measured how much force he could bear and sent him back to small motions when he tried to skip them. His pain changed from sharp to dull. It did not vanish.

He cut his hair one afternoon because the loose tie kept catching on the training collar. The woman who did it asked how short. He held his hand above his ears and then wished he had kept one lock longer when he saw himself afterward. Ypin said it made him look like every other recruit who had lost an argument with a collar. Tobious asked whether Ypin had ever won that argument. Ypin touched his own cropped head and conceded he had not.

At a market under the service towers, Ypin bought them two thin cakes filled with sour fruit. Tobious had never tasted the fruit. Its sharpness made him pull a face so emphatic that the seller laughed. He bought a second cake to decide whether he liked it. Ypin said the fact that he had to check was answer enough.

For half an hour they sat at the edge of a public garden while water ran beneath the paving. Tobious described how a fig tasted when it had been stolen too early from a branch, and how Mariya could tell whose hand had taken it by the cut on the stem. Ypin told him which stalls in the service ward kept prices steady when the upper concourse ran short. Neither tried to turn the conversation toward the root. Tobious found that he had wanted such an hour more than he had known.

The next day Suss put a powered practice blade in his left hand. It was weighted like a working weapon but would shut down before it could cut flesh. Tobious expected the field to make the old motions easier. It made them more exacting. Contact had to be kept through movement, his own shield would drop, and a partner had to be in place before he committed.

He failed by rushing. He failed by waiting so long that the opponent moved. He failed once because he watched the blade and forgot where the exit lay. Suss stopped that attempt and took him to the room's edge.

“I can teach you a faster strike,” he said. “It will not put an exit behind you. Your assignment is to get the people at the door home.”

“I know the assignment.”

“Then show me.”

Tobious disliked the words. They resembled something Mariya would have said, and therefore gave him no one to blame for hearing them.

***

On the sixteenth day, Panjios gave the group a timed exercise. The painted room stood in for a damaged receiver. Jorra and Sennos would operate the protective line; Ypin controlled a fixed shield stand at the exit; Tobious's task was to approach, make one guarded cut at a marked point, and withdraw with two civilians represented by weighted frames.

The first run failed before the cut. Tobious had calculated the distance to the marked point and left too little space for the first frame to turn. The training alarm sounded while he was still forcing it around a corner.

“They're people,” Jorra said when they reset. “People don't become thinner because your diagram requires it.”

She showed him how to angle the frame and where a stretcher bearer would need to stand. Sennos checked the line length. Ypin moved the shield stand nearer to the exit, but refused Tobious's request to place it at the marked point.

“It will lose the coverage we need at the door,” he said. “I'm not putting everyone behind you without cover so your cut looks clean.”

Tobious started to argue, then saw the uncovered strip Ypin meant. He altered his approach.

On the second run he waited for Jorra's signal, went in low, and held the blade against the marked point. One heartbeat, two, three. His own protection dropped. The empty air at his back felt suddenly very wide. Sennos stepped where they had agreed he would. The cut registered. Tobious withdrew.

An opposing trainee fell back, disarmed by the exercise field. Tobious took one step toward him, wanting to secure the room. He saw the first weighted frame still waiting beside the exit. He let the opponent go, took the frame's handle, and helped Jorra move it behind Ypin's cover. Sennos brought the second.

The alarm remained silent until all five were out.

Tobious expected triumph. What he felt first was relief that nobody had needed him to be brilliant. He had been where he said he would be. The others had been where they said they would be. The room had not rewarded courage; it had responded to a working arrangement.

“You gave up the chase,” Panjios said.

“The exit was the assignment.”

Panjios nodded. “That part was good.”

Suss checked the recorded timing. “Your cut is still slow. Against a trained opponent, Jorra could not stand open for you that long.”

“I know.”

“Tomorrow we make it shorter.”

Panjios handed Tobious a new authorization card. He explained its marks before Tobious could ask: permission to observe the relic demonstration in Root Hall, not permission to carry the blade. Tobious looked at the three-line seal on the card. The same sign had appeared on Darran's drawing. In Veyr it opened doors; in Aras it had put men under the hill.

He slid the card into his pocket. He wanted to see what lay beyond that next door. He wanted, just as strongly, to know who had decided where the seal could be used.
