# SCHLELBERG

## Chapter Six — A City That Does Not Sleep

The physician asked Tobious to put both hands on the table. He put down the left. The right stayed against his chest.

“That one too,” she said.

Panjios had spent the walk to the clinic translating for him. Now a small curved piece rested behind Tobious's ear, and the woman's words arrived in Aras speech a fraction after her mouth moved. She pointed at the device when she noticed his glance.

“It will miss some words,” she said. “Ask when it does. My name is Edda.”

Tobious offered his right hand. Edda did not take it until he nodded. She turned it gently, studied the swelling and bruising, then passed a wand above his wrist. An image appeared on a pane beside her: pale bones suspended inside a dark shape that had the outline of his own arm.

He jerked back.

“It's a picture,” she said. “You can put your arm down if you'd rather.”

He looked from the pane to the wrist. “How did it get inside?”

“It didn't. I can show you with mine.”

She scanned her own forearm. Another image appeared. Its small bones moved when she flexed her hand. He tried his fingers, and watched their counterparts stir on the pane. The city outside had roads in the sky; here it displayed a man's hidden skeleton without cutting him. Wonder was beginning to feel like labor. Every marvel needed learning before he could decide whether to trust it.

“No break,” Edda said. “The tendons need rest. This bruising along the ribs is less pleasing.”

She examined the place where the soldier's shield had struck him and cleaned the cut at his lip. The treatment stung, though less than the damp cloth Mariya had used. Edda sealed the cut with a clear dressing that clung without thread.

“I need the wrist in a support for several days. After that, movement a little at a time. A sword is out of the question until I say otherwise.”

“I didn't say I wanted one.”

“Panjios brought you through the Wardens' entrance. People often do.”

She turned to write something on a tablet. Through the open door Tobious saw a woman in work clothes arguing with a clerk. A young man sat beside her with a burned palm pressed to his chest. The clerk kept pointing at a list. Edda set her tablet down and went out to them.

“The palm first,” she told the clerk. “The registration can wait.”

“The service company hasn't certified him.”

“His hand has.”

The clerk stepped aside. Edda brought the man to a wash station, then asked the woman how the burn had happened. He was not a Warden, not a visitor with Panjios. His care began before anyone established which office should pay for it.

When Edda returned, Tobious asked whether she worked for Panjios.

“For the clinic and the service ward. We take Concord patients because people arrive injured from Concord work. That doesn't give the Concord my records unless a patient permits it.”

The translation piece rendered her last words slowly. He asked her to say them again. She did, and marked his injury certificate with a separate civic seal.

“This also lets me request a casualty review if an incident puts residents at risk,” she said. “It doesn't mean every commander does as I ask.”

Tobious believed that. He had met commanders.

***

Ypin arrived with a tool roll and an expression that suggested he would have preferred a broken pipe.

“I inventory field exposure,” he said. “Anything you touched, anything that touched you, how long the route was active.”

“Edda has already looked at me.”

“I'm not here to look at your bones.”

He set a slim recorder on the table and waited. Tobious did not pick it up. Ypin was perhaps a year younger than he was, with cropped hair and a jacket repaired at one elbow. Its pockets carried the small weight of tools. He had the fingers of someone used to reaching into narrow places.

“You sound angry,” Tobious said.

“I have three transport pumps waiting because a station that shouldn't have been commissioned woke under your village.”

“Then blame whoever commissioned it.”

Ypin considered him. “I do.”

It was a more useful answer than Tobious had expected.

They began with the lower chamber. Ypin asked how the water had stood, which hand Tobious had used, where his blood had touched the frame, how long he had heard the first hum. Tobious could answer some questions exactly. Others dissolved when he tried to distinguish one frightened heartbeat from the next.

“You don't know the duration?” Ypin asked.

“I knew the water was going down.”

“That is a condition, not a duration.”

“And it was the condition that mattered to us.”

Ypin looked at his recorder. “Fair.”

The translation piece faltered whenever he named a part of the root. He stopped using the terms, drew a quick picture instead, and showed how three surface sites joined one lower line. Tobious recognized the village frame, the shore cave, and a third place uphill among old stone workings.

“This is the Quarry,” Ypin said. “We can service it. We cannot build you a fourth place to stand.”

Tobious asked why a machine buried beneath Aras could have a repair station in Veyr. Ypin showed him a layered drawing: one outline of the bay and ridge, another fitted over it, with roads and towers where the old terraces had been.

“Same ground,” he said. “Twenty-six hundred years between the two readings.”

Tobious did not know the marks representing the number. Ypin made groups on the table with Edda's spare counters: twenty-six stones, each one standing for a hundred harvests. They covered the length of the table. Tobious ran a finger beside them and stopped halfway.

His mother had once stood on the terrace where the Civic Spine rose. Twenty-six hundred winters had passed over that piece of ground. The stone might have been moved, ground to dust, built into another wall. He could not measure such time by walking it.

“Does anyone remember Aras?” he asked.

“There are records,” Ypin said. “Some names. Less than you'd want.”

Tobious looked at him sharply. Ypin met his gaze.

“I meant the records are incomplete. I wasn't saying the people were.”

He finished the inventory without trying to make Tobious less uneasy about the distance. Before leaving, he adjusted the translation piece so that it sat more securely behind Tobious's ear.

“It will understand common speech,” he said. “Labels are another matter. If a door tells you something, ask a person before walking through.”

“You said you had pumps waiting.”

“They've waited this long.”

The corner of Ypin's mouth moved, barely.

***

On the fourth day, Panjios took Tobious beyond the clinic. Edda had cleared him to walk, provided he kept the wrist supported and rested when his ribs began to hurt. Tobious nodded at the instruction in her presence and broke it within an hour by trying to climb a service stair two steps at a time. The ribs reminded him before Panjios did.

They crossed a public terrace beneath a roof that changed its shade as the sun moved. Food grew behind clear walls: climbing beans, leaves the size of his hands, roots suspended in moving water. In Aras they counted jars because a bad season could leave empty fields. Here people cultivated above the road, yet a line at the food counter stretched around the terrace.

At one end of the terrace a musician played a stringed instrument whose sound moved through the paving in low pulses. A small crowd had gathered around her, some eating while they listened, others waiting with empty bowls. Across the open walk, a cleaner was replacing a cracked transparent tile with a tool that lifted it without touching the people passing on either side. The city gave the impression of having solved every old difficulty from a distance. Up close, it held a woman trying to keep a tune steady over the noise of repairs, and a worker asking pedestrians to give him enough room to do a careful job.

Tobious tried to buy a portion for the musician. The counter would not accept the old bronze bead he had carried from home. Panjios paid from his service account and then showed Tobious how the public meal credit worked. Visitors received a small allotment until they were registered; residents earned and exchanged more through their work and civic share. The system was generous enough to feed someone who had just crossed from Aras. It was also a system he could not use without a translator and a person willing to explain its marks.

“Why are they waiting?” Tobious asked.

“A reservoir in the lower ward is down,” Panjios said. “Those growers will use more water to keep the roots cool. Some kitchens are cutting portions until the service teams finish.”

“All that green, and a broken pump still makes people hungry.”

“Yes.”

At a transit junction, a warning sounded. It was not the soft delay from Tobious's first carriage ride. A service platform had stopped above an open maintenance shaft, its underside caught on a bent guide. Three workers were stranded inside. Below them lay a walkway crowded with people trying to move away. The platform lurched as its drive tried to reset.

A man in a dark field jacket crossed the junction at a run. He looked once at the platform, once at the guide, and began giving orders. Two Wardens formed a line at the lower walk, directing people back. Another brought a shield unit to the shaft's edge. The man's voice carried through the noise without rising.

“Cut the drive. Manual hold at the west rail. No one underneath it.”

Panjios moved to the rail. “Suss.”

The commander glanced at him, then at Tobious. “Keep your visitor behind cover.”

Tobious disliked the word *visitor* enough to follow the order slowly. He saw why when the platform jerked again. A length of guide peeled away and struck the floor where he had been standing.

One of the Wardens set a shimmering field between the falling fragments and the people retreating along the walk. The field caught the first piece. The second struck beside it and showered them with dust. The Warden braced herself; the shield had changed the force, not removed it.

“Shield line, how much heat?” Suss called.

“One more hit this size. Then I move.”

“Move before it.”

He climbed a narrow service ladder toward the platform. A shield gathered around him, so faint Tobious had to find its edge against the flashing warning lights. Another Warden held a line from below. Ypin appeared at a maintenance panel with his tool roll already open.

“The west latch isn't taking a command,” Ypin shouted. “The bracket is bent through the socket.”

“Manual access?”

“One span above you. The hatch opens inward.”

Suss reached it. The platform tilted. He caught a rail, pulled himself to the hatch, and called to the people inside. They answered, frightened but coherent. He gave them one instruction at a time: move to the east wall, secure the loose case, put their hands where he could see them when the hatch opened.

He drew a short blade and set it against the twisted latch. His shield vanished. The line operator stepped closer below, lifting his own field toward the exposed commander. Three heartbeats passed as Suss held the blade in place. The latch parted. The platform shifted hard enough to drive his shoulder into the frame.

“One at a time,” he said.

He brought the first worker through and clipped her to the line. The operator lowered her. The second came with a cut above his eye. The third had a trapped ankle and could not move. Suss did not pull him free by force. He asked Ypin whether the drive could hold still for thirty breaths.

“Twenty,” Ypin said. “I'll give you twenty and then you have to get clear.”

“Make it nineteen.”

He reentered the platform with the blade and freed the pinning bracket. When he passed the worker to the operator, a warning heat shimmer crossed Suss's shield and went red at the edges. He dropped down the ladder rather than pretending it would take another blow.

The platform sagged onto its manual hold after he reached the floor. No one cheered until the third worker was on a stretcher. Then the sound came in fragments, from people who had seen someone they knew come back alive.

Tobious went to help move a loose barrier from the evacuation route. His wrist resisted. The shield operator took the heavier end without comment, letting him carry the light one. Suss noticed them as he checked the rescued man's ankle.

“You came through Salt yesterday,” he said to Tobious.

“Yes.”

“Your hand's still injured. Don't make my operator explain to your physician why you used it here.”

The operator made a sound that might have been a laugh. Suss took off his shield unit and handed it to a technician.

“The charge is spent,” he said. “Replace it before the next lift. A shield you cannot trust should be called a lamp.”

He walked beside the stretcher until the clinic transport arrived. Tobious had seen him risk his life for workers who looked nothing like commanders. That did not prove what kind of man Suss was in every matter. It proved he knew how to bring people down from a failing platform.

***

On the fifth day, Panjios and Suss brought Tobious to a room overlooking the coast. Ypin was there to run a map. He stood apart from the two Wardens, with the posture of a man who expected to be asked for numbers rather than judgment.

The map rose from the table in layers. Tobious found the old ridge only after Panjios turned off the future roads. Three points burned in the ancient landscape: beneath Aras, beside the salt shore, and in the quarry hills. A single line joined them to a point under Veyr's Civic Spine.

“One root,” Panjios said. “Three places to reach it from your side.”

“The one beneath Aras is broken.”

“Damaged,” Ypin corrected. “It still takes power. That's why yesterday's thing could come through.”

“It didn't stay,” Tobious said. “Why?”

Ypin spread his hands, searching for an ordinary comparison. Suss answered first.

“It can hold a shape near an active route. When you collapsed that route, it lost the current that held it here. It retreated before it came apart.”

Tobious remembered the creature drawing itself back toward the fading light. What he had taken for fear had been need.

“If the door opens again, others can come.”

“Yes,” Suss said. “And the root is beginning to lean on your contact to remain steady. We need you to learn how to hold it while we repair the damaged receiver.”

“Lean where?” Tobious asked.

Ypin separated the two outlines of the bay with his hands, then let their edges touch. “When the damaged root cannot keep the eras apart, the pressures in one begin to reach the other. A wall can shift where no wall should be. A service line can carry heat from a room that hasn't been built yet. The old records call the runaway overlap Schlelberg.”

Tobious tried the word once. It told him nothing about the stone under his mother's field. “And the Iwang?”

“They use the gradients between the volumes,” Ypin said. “A live seam lets them take shape on a side they could not otherwise reach. The overlap gives them more seams.”

The map had three bright points. Tobious thought of a fourth appearing where Mariya slept, or where the city held its gardens. A word for the process did not make it less frightening. It made it possible to ask which part of it they had actually measured.

“And if I won't?”

“Then we have worse options. I would rather make a trained operator than a trapped man.”

Panjios showed him the first training schedule: care for the wrist, observation, controlled movement, then contact work. Nothing on the page promised a sword before he could close his hand without pain.

“What about the people living near the other two places?” Tobious asked. “If you open them, do they know?”

Suss touched the Salt point. “This route operates under an arrangement with coastal authorities. Openings are logged and limited. The quarry is surveyed before use.”

“And Aras?”

“Its receiver should not have been woken without a guarded crew,” Panjios said. “Darran's commission is under review.”

Tobious watched the three lights. The map showed land and routes, not homes. He tried to place Mariya's granary, Samon's sister's house, Lios's aunt. Their village was a bright point no larger than his fingernail.

“I want messages going both ways,” he said. “If the work in Aras changes, she needs to know. If the village counts people your map doesn't, I need to know.”

Ypin looked at him for the first time as though a question had been asked of him rather than about him.

“The shore relay can carry a small packet on its service window,” he said. “I can show you what fits.”

Suss nodded. “Within the safety schedule. And if the village site shifts, I want the report as quickly as you do.”

Tobious believed that, too. He had watched Suss work under a falling platform. He had learned enough in five days to know that wanting to save people did not make anyone's numbers complete.

“Then I'll train,” he said. “Tell me where I begin.”
