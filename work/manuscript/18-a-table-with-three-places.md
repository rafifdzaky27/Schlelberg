# SCHLELBERG

## Chapter Eighteen — A Table with Three Places

The relay opened with the sound of water in a stone channel.

Tobious leaned toward the speaking surface before a voice came through. A small indicator above it gave them a fixed interval. Ypin had explained that the Storehouse branch could spare no more power today. The contact would end when the mark reached the far side, whether or not they had finished saying what mattered.

“Tobi?”

Mariya's voice was thin and uneven, but it was hers. He closed his eyes for a moment. He could see her on the granary steps with flour at her temple. When he opened them, the relay light was already moving.

“I'm here. Are you hurt?”

“Tired. Forty-seven of us are alive. Thesia died. Did you tell her sister?”

“The porter carried the name. I asked him to have her told about the children too.”

There was a brief gap. A clicking sound crossed the line from the annex, one high note and two low. Mariya waited for it to pass before speaking.

“We need more clean water. The ration hatch opens, but not enough for washing. The landing is real. The tether won't let us use it.”

“I've seen the map. We are trying a faster release setting.”

“Who is *we*?”

“Suss has the route. Panjios and Ypin are checking it. Edda is watching the injuries.”

“And what is the route?”

He told her about the selective extraction. It could free one person early by distributing strain through the others and a temporary sink. He did not say her name until she asked whether Suss had chosen someone.

“He said you could come first.”

“Did he say who carries what leaves with me?”

“He says the added strain is below a monitored threshold.”

“That isn't a name.”

“I know.”

“Then I can't agree.”

He gripped the edge of the table. “You can get out of there. We can ask after you're safe.”

“I want to get out,” she said. Her voice sharpened. “Do you think I don't? I want to sleep where the wall doesn't click. I want to eat bread without watching whether Lios has had his. I want to see you standing in a doorway without wondering if the floor will move between us. I will not buy that by making the others hold a cost no one has explained to them.”

He had prepared for noble refusal. Her anger made him hear the desire inside it. She was not choosing danger because she loved suffering. She was choosing not to make another person unknowingly bear her escape.

“I thought if I could show you a door,” he said, “you would let me open it.”

“Show me what it does to the room. Then we can decide.”

The indicator crossed its midpoint. Tobious wanted to spend the rest of the interval calculating. Mariya had already sent measurements. He could not give her a safe date today. He could tell her something else without pretending it solved the count.

“I missed you at supper,” he said.

She was quiet. He heard movement around her, someone asking for a jar, then her answer to them. She returned to the relay.

“What did you eat?”

“A cake Ypin made. I had to fry it again.”

“Of course you did.”

“He says the fruit they put inside it is meant to be sour.”

“You bought a second one anyway, didn't you?”

He looked through the booth glass at Ypin, who had turned discreetly away. “I did.”

“Tell me something else that isn't a machine,” she said.

He looked past Ypin to the evening beyond the booth. “There is a woman in the lower ward who grows bitter greens on her window rail. She trades them for broken cups because her grandson makes the handles into little boats.”

“Does she know you?”

“She says I use too much water to wash a pan.”

“Then I believe she knows you.”

He wanted to describe the floating gardens and the luminous streets, all the things that had made Veyr seem beyond any place she could imagine. Instead he told her how the woman guarded her greens from gulls and how Ypin's kitchen plants leaned toward a warm service pipe. Those details could belong to a life she might choose, if they reached one. He would not use them to sell the city to her now.

Mariya laughed. The sound was small, frayed by the line, and more welcome than any reassurance he had received since leaving her.

He remembered a flood in the year they were both old enough to be trusted with tools and too young to be trusted with a gate. Samon had sent them to watch the upper channel. Rain turned the path to clay before they reached it. Tobious had insisted he could force the swollen sluice wheel; Mariya found the silted outlet that made the wheel resist. They came home wet, carrying half a basket of mud and no heroic story to tell. At supper she had eaten his portion of figs because he was busy explaining how close the wall had come to failing. He had thought of her first whenever a channel ran wrong after that. The habit had lasted longer than any flood.

“What are you smiling at?” she asked.

“The year you stole my figs after the upper gate flooded.”

“You gave them to me while you talked. Your memory is unreliable.”

“Then we will need a witness when we finally have that week.”

The line carried her breathing for a moment. Neither treated the imagined week as a contract the world had signed for them. It was something they still wanted.

“I keep thinking of the sluice,” she said. “After rain, when the stone has warmed by noon. It makes that hollow noise before the east gate opens.”

“Samon says the mason's brother still turns it too early.”

“Samon says that about everyone.”

“He said it about me, and he was right.”

“I want a meal without counting portions,” she said. “I want to sit long enough to finish it. With you.”

The ordinary wish filled the booth more completely than a promise of rescue. Tobious had imagined being strong enough to offer her a future. He had not imagined she would tell him what kind of future she wanted and that he might simply listen.

“I love you,” he said.

She breathed in. For one fearful heartbeat he thought the line had failed.

He had imagined saying it on the shore path in Aras, with no one listening and no fixed interval. He had waited for a day in which the words would not sound like another promise he could not keep. The dial was still moving. He said them because they were true inside this day, with its broken plan and narrow line.

“I love you too, Tobi. It isn't why I stay with them. It is why I need you to understand me while I do.”

The indicator approached its end. Tobious told her what he could promise: he would work for all forty-seven, ask who carried each proposed cost, and send an honest timetable when one existed. He could not promise success. She asked for water, a release sequence, and the truth if the window narrowed. She did not ask him to become fearless. He did not ask her to wait for him as proof of love.

“The relay closes in ten breaths,” Ypin said from behind the glass.

Mariya heard him. “Tell him I can hear him counting.”

Ypin lifted a hand. Tobious saw Mariya in the gesture: her insistence on making another person visible even from a sealed room.

They used the remaining breaths to say the names of two people who needed care. Then the line quieted. The indicator reached its end exactly. No necessary confession was stolen by a convenient failure. Everything they had said remained unfinished because their lives were.

***

Ypin's kitchen had three chairs because a colleague sometimes came to share a meal after a late shift. Tonight the third held Tobious's coat and the record copy from the archive. He moved both to the floor before sitting. It was a small act; he felt foolish noticing it.

Ypin warmed bread in a shallow pan. “You argued with her.”

“She argued with me.”

“Did she change your mind?”

“She made me say what my mind was. I hadn't done that.”

“What was it?”

“I wanted her out even if I couldn't explain the cost. I thought because I loved her the answer would be obvious to her too.”

Ypin turned the bread before it burned. “It was obvious. She wants to live. She also wants the others asked.”

“I heard her.”

“Then that was a useful argument.”

Ypin put the bread between them. The plants by the warm pipe had grown since Tobious last ate here. One leaf had turned brown at the edge, but the rest leaned toward the window. Ypin checked the water in its dish before taking his own food.

“You keep offering yourself for every dangerous part,” he said. “Then you get angry when someone else wants a say in the danger.”

“The root chose my body.”

“The root acquired your signal. It doesn't make you responsible for every hand in the room.”

Tobious tore a piece of bread. “If I let someone else take the risk and they die, I will still have to live with it.”

“Yes,” Ypin said. “So will they if you take it and die. The point of asking is not to make the cost disappear.”

They ate for a while. Tobious looked at the record beside his coat. Ypin's signature on the newer biological-load form had traveled with the copy to Edda. The technician had not asked for it to be hidden.

“What happened to the forms you signed?” Tobious asked.

“I traced some of them. Most were routine loads. Some had the same neural flags as the people under Aras. I don't know how many were people. I should have asked eighteen months ago.”

“You told me you hadn't proved it.”

“At the time I hadn't. I also knew enough to be worried. I liked the idea that if I collected a perfect record, someone else would decide what to do with it.”

Tobious thought of his own signed trial authorization. He had demanded measurements of injury to his hand. He had not demanded the names behind *network load*. Each man had found a way to make a missing answer belong to a later day.

“We'll ask now,” Tobious said.

“And if they refuse?”

“Then the refusal becomes part of the record.”

Ypin took the third chair and pulled it closer to the table. He set the authenticated copy on it so they could compare its dates with the current trial plan. It was a practical surface. For an instant Tobious imagined Mariya sitting there, objecting to how late they were eating and taking the crisp edge from his bread. He let the image be pleasant without using it to decide for her.

The copy listed when the network had taken power, not whose nerves had steadied it. Ypin showed Tobious the missing column, then showed the form bearing his own signature. He did not push it aside when their hands reached the same page. “I am going to ask for the full path again,” he said. “This time I will let Edda know if they refuse.”

“And Suss?”

“He will know when Edda files the refusal.”

Tobious thought of the trial he had signed. He could withdraw his name from it before dawn. He wanted to wait for one more reading. Ypin could see the delay in his face. He did not argue Tobious out of it over bread; he left the form where they would both have to look at it when the meal was done.
