# SCHLELBERG

## Chapter Twenty-Nine — What Love Cannot Keep

After the count, Tobious and Mariya found a place to sit where no one needed either of them for a few minutes.

It took them an hour to believe the count would hold. Tobious helped carry one man to a cart and found the man's sister already arguing with a carrier about the safest road. Mariya followed Nerissa through the shelter, matching a child's name to the aunt who had come for him. When she returned, she looked at the list again. Forty-three marks. Forty-three voices, sleeping or calling for water or speaking to people who had waited above. She had lived with the possibility of one name disappearing whenever she closed her eyes. Even now, each time someone moved behind a curtain she expected to turn the page and find a new absence.

“Nerissa has it,” Tobious said when Mariya reached for the list a third time.

“I know.”

He did not take the board from her. He waited while she handed it to Nerissa herself. On the far side of the yard, Samon was telling a carrier exactly where a cart wheel would sink. The work continued without either of them. Mariya let Tobious lead her inside.

The shelter belonged to the fisherman who had opened his net yard to the coastal carriers. His family had moved the hooks from the wall, and the room smelled of rope, salt, and the barley soup being made for the survivors. Forty-three people from the annex were alive. All had a carer or a place to sleep. The wider village was still moving, and the root remained open. For this interval, no one asked Tobious to hold a blade or Mariya to read a name.

They shared a bowl. Mariya's hands shook when she lifted the spoon. Tobious's fingers, stiff from the sword and the stretcher boards, fumbled the handle, so they passed it back and forth with their lefts and laughed once at the poor coordination. The laugh ended without turning into tears. Neither of them had the strength for a perfect account of the day.

Mariya told him about the five who had not come out. She began with Thesia, whose sister's children had eaten because Thesia had asked for them by name. Pehl had kept a stone in his pocket to test the sound of clay before firing it. Annet had complained about water and then taught Hessah to mend the skins. Orran had held a brace after learning what could fail. Hessah had asked for salt air and died before the wind turned. Tobious listened. The names were not a count he could use to make his loss look proportionate to the forty-three who had survived.

“I cut without asking what happened to the far floor,” he said. “I saw the warning. I did it anyway.”

“I know. I was there.”

“I'm sorry.”

“I believe you.” She set down the spoon. “That doesn't make Thesia alive. It matters that you can say what you did without telling me the twelve saved make it right.”

He nodded. He had feared she would offer forgiveness before he could bear it, or refuse him entirely. She gave him an account he could live beside without owning her judgment.

“I told people help would come by a certain interval,” she said. “I didn't know it. I wanted them to keep sleeping and drinking and waiting. When the interval passed, I had made their fear worse. I also spent seed without asking.”

“You fed them.”

“Yes. Don't use that to make the secrecy good.”

He smiled faintly. “You sound like Ypin.”

“Then he must be tired of you too.”

Tobious told her Panjios had died at the manual door. He described the two workers who crossed because the older man held it, and Suss's blade. He told her about the ninety-seven Panjios had abandoned years before and the false account he had left in the records.

“Ypin signed one of the later forms,” Tobious added. “He told me before he knew I would find it. He has been trying to put that signature beside the real count.”

Mariya wiped the edge of the bowl with a piece of bread. “Will they let him?”

“Edda has copies outside the command archive. He knows where the rest were kept.”

“Then don't let Panjios become only the man who died well, or Ypin only the one who helped you afterward.”

Tobious heard the care and the anger in her voice. She was asking him to remember whole lives, including what they had cost other people. He had learned the shape of that request in a room where Panjios could no longer answer it.

Mariya had met Panjios while he delivered grain and the brace for Samon. She remembered how he asked Samon before lifting the injured leg. “A person can be gentle with one man and cruel with a hundred whose faces he won't have to see,” she said. “And he can change what he does at the next door without undoing the first.”

Tobious touched the scar beneath his left eye. “I wanted him to live long enough for both things to be heard.”

“You can make sure they are.”

He reached for her hand. She turned hers to meet it, and they sat with the bowl cooling between them.

***

The shelter quieted after midnight. Mariya lay beside Tobious beneath a wool blanket. They had slept near each other before as children at harvest gatherings, with other households crowded around. They had never had this much silence together as adults. He could hear her breathing when it slowed, then her turning when a cart passed on the road outside.

Between the carts came the small sounds of a village trying to make a night: a ladle striking the bottom of a pot, an infant unwilling to sleep in a strange room, a man outside singing one line of a work song and losing the words. Mariya knew the line. She sang the next one quietly. Tobious joined where he could, badly. They stopped before anyone in the yard heard them and began to request more. When she turned toward him, her face was warm from the blanket and still hollow with fatigue. He wanted every ordinary night they had never been given.

“Come with me,” he said.

He had promised himself he would offer the choice without trying to sell it. The words came out bare because he wanted the answer too much to decorate them.

“I know I can,” she said.

“There is a room in Ypin's ward. Edda could help with work. You would hate some of the food.”

“You would fry it again.”

“Until it tasted like something.”

“Would I be allowed to work?” she asked. “Not as proof that I deserve the room. Work I choose.”

“Yes. Edda would insist on rest first, and you'd argue with her.”

“She sounds like Nerissa.”

“She is less patient.”

Mariya laughed, then grew serious. “Would people know what I came from?”

“Some would. You could tell the others what you wanted.”

“And when they learned I didn't save everyone?”

He could not promise a city without judgment. He had seen how quickly Suss's notice had turned a living operator into a public danger. “They would hear the true count if we kept it,” he said. “I would stand beside you while they did.”

She touched his cheek, near the scar but not on it. “That is a life I can imagine.”

She took time to answer. He did not fill it with arguments. In the dark he imagined her in Veyr: learning the signs, finding the households a register had missed, telling him when he had mistaken strength for help. It was not only his fantasy. She had wanted to see the hanging streets, to learn whether the sea looked the same beneath them, to live where a clinic could set a crushed leg more gently than Nerissa had been able to set Samon's.

“I could make a life there,” she said. “I don't think I would vanish. Part of me wants to find out who I would be.”

“Then come.”

“Another part wants to remain where the water from our hills reaches the bay. I want to see what we can make of Aras with the soldiers drawn back. I want to argue with the neighbors about the seed and hear them argue back. I want to be old enough to know whether Lios learns to carry a cup without being ordered to.”

“Therren has not agreed to the charter yet,” he said.

“I know. He may agree and break it. Some of the people who oppose him will still disagree with me about whose grain should be planted first.” She turned the blanket back from her shoulders. “I am not choosing a peaceful village that doesn't exist. I am choosing to be here while they make one, or fail and try again.”

He understood then why telling her she was free of duty would not move her. She had already let other people take parts of the work. What remained was a desire, not a sentence she had imposed on herself. He wished it led toward him. He loved that she could say where it led without asking him to approve.

“You don't have to stay to keep them alive.”

“I know. Nerissa took the store list. The carriers know the roads. The village will live without me if I go. That is why I can choose it honestly.”

He lay awake beside her. He could ask again. He could describe Veyr's light, the soft floor in Edda's clinic, their possible dinners. She already knew enough to desire some of it. There was no extra fact that would turn her choice into a mistake.

“I wanted a week with you when all we counted was figs,” he said.

“So did I.”

“I still do.”

She pressed his hand to her chest. “I love you. I am going to stay.”

The two sentences hurt because neither cancelled the other. He drew her close, careful of her bruises and of the cut at his own eye. They remained awake for a while, learning the shape of a life they would not have. No one made that life less lovely so leaving it would be easier.

Before they slept, they named the constraints once. He had to return to the root seam in Veyr. The cut needed his living contact for fifteen heartbeats. All three ancient accesses shared the mineral reference. The seven idle days for transfer could not fit between the final release and the measured Day-84 rise. She could cross now, or remain. After the cut, neither could use a spare door to undo the decision.

“I don't want you to think I stayed because you were less than home,” she said.

“I won't.”

“And you aren't leaving because you love me less.”

“I know.”

“You wanted to come back,” she said. “I saw it when you stood at the pane.”

“I did. I still do.”

“Then let that be true without making the cut a punishment for wanting it.”

He looked at the bandage on his hand. He had thought guilt might make the final action easier to accept, as though a man who had harmed others should be grateful for a task that could consume him. It did not. He wanted to live. He wanted her. He also knew who would stand beneath a new surge if he turned away from the root. The choice was harder because he expected to survive it and keep wanting what he could not have.

They did not promise to understand every day that followed. Tonight they understood enough.

***

The walk to Salt Gate began while the sky was still dark. Jorra and Sennos carried their field gear. Their work covering the ancient rescue was complete; they had to reach the future Root Hall before Tobious for the final operation. Mariya walked beside him without asking them to slow.

At the cave mouth, Tobious checked the binding around his right wrist. It had loosened while he carried a stretcher, and the strain from the old injury and the blade had made the joint swell again. Mariya took the cloth and tied it the way she had behind the granary on the morning he first left: firm enough to hold, loose where the swelling hurt. Her hands remembered the knot.

“There,” she said.

“You could have taught me.”

“I tried.”

He had no answer to that. He held her face with his left hand and kissed her. Neither tried to make the touch last as long as the years it would have to replace.

Jorra crossed first, then Sennos. The Salt opening showed the receiving room in Veyr with unmistakable moving air. Tobious looked at Mariya. Behind her, the coastal path ran back to the forty-two other survivors and to a village negotiating what it would become. He wanted to know whether he would live through the final cut. He could not ask her to carry his uncertainty as a reason to change her choice.

“Go finish it,” she said.

He stepped into Veyr. Before the field narrowed, he turned once. She stood on the salt-white stone, not waving, seeing him leave. The opening remained physical and open for one more breath. Then the receiving schedule closed it, and the air of Aras no longer moved across his face.
