# SCHLELBERG

## Chapter Thirty-Two — What Is Kept

The first autumn rain arrived while Mariya was reading the relief charter.

Therren's scribe had pressed it into clay that morning, under the captain's seal and the marks of household witnesses from Aras and three neighboring villages. The terms were limited: the garrison would leave the village houses and withdraw to the ridge, the remaining ordinary labor detainees would be released, and the harvest levy would be reduced for the season. Telassar still held the citadel and the road beyond it. A future captain might contest the charter when the weather changed.

For now, Therren needed the carriers who had stopped moving grain and oil through the harbor. His secret surveyor was dead. The ancient Receiver had become silent stone, and the soldiers who had worked beside it wanted no part of another opening. The concession came from their costs and the neighboring villages' refusal to carry them alone. Mariya accepted it for what it was, not for the conversion of a man who had once called their seed the king's measure.

The negotiation had taken place in the yard where his soldiers first weighed the jars. Therren wanted the carriers to resume at once and offered to remove only the men quartered inside the houses. A boatbuilder's daughter asked whether the workers held at the ridge counted as people inside a house. The captain said they were subject to a separate labor order. The neighboring carriers pushed their unloaded carts into the road and said the distinction would matter when he tried to feed his garrison.

One of Therren's soldiers had a brother among the ridge laborers. He did not speak in the negotiation. When the captain called for the labor tablet, the soldier found it quickly and placed it where the household witnesses could read the names. Therren saw the gesture and said nothing. His men had watched Darran die beside a worksite they had been told was safe to guard. Their obedience had limits now, though those limits might change again under a different commander. Mariya did not ask the soldier to become a symbol of a kinder occupation. She checked his brother's name against her board.

Mariya had brought the names from the old clay tablets. She could not read every palace mark, but she could put each household beside the person missing from it. Therren recognized several as useful craftsmen. He wanted their labor available for the autumn repairs. The carriers wanted those hands at the boats and mills that supplied the district. He agreed to release the ordinary detainees under a seasonal charter, keeping a smaller detail at the ridge to protect the road. No one called it justice. It changed where the men would sleep that night.

When the scribe wrote the levy relief, he used a measure without stating its size. Mariya put the old wooden measure on the table. Therren glanced at it, then had the dimensions pressed into the clay. One band of wood had once taken a year's seed from them. She would not leave a new band to the next scribe's discretion.

She read the charter aloud in the meeting yard with Nerissa beside her. She did not tell the households it made them safe forever. The scribe had made a copy for the ridge; two village witnesses would hold another. The carrier households would report if the old toll returned. Samon wanted a mark added for workers still kept at the citadel; Therren's scribe resisted until one of the neighboring carriers named the road he would close again. The mark was added.

“We'll need to check it after the next levy,” Mariya said. “If they take more, bring the seal and the names here before anyone tries to settle it alone.”

The boatbuilder's daughter asked who would keep the copy if Mariya was away. Mariya gave it to three household witnesses to hold in separate jars, each marked in the meeting record. The carriers promised to compare the ridge copy at the next market. The charter would survive only if more than one person knew how to challenge it. Mariya had learned that a good count could be made powerless by keeping it in one careful hand.

There was no cheer. People discussed who would watch the road, which boats would bring grain before the rains made the northern path difficult, and whether a family could move back into a house the soldiers had used as a kitchen. Their questions sounded like the village recovering its voice. They did not sound like peace, and Mariya did not require them to.

***

Forty-three people had come out of the annex alive. Aras held many more than forty-three, and the returned captives did not stand apart as a single body. Some had gone back to households where fields needed work; others slept longer or started at every sudden click. One woman preferred the shore to the ancestor terrace and spent her mornings helping mend nets. The shelter lists were revised as people changed their minds about where they wished to be.

Nerissa resumed her visits to mothers and children. She kept a careful watch on those whose legs or hands still failed them after the field surges. She could not cure every injury by naming it; she could make sure no one disappeared from care because a list was finished. Samon taught the younger workers how to set a sluice gate without twisting the damaged left leg beneath him. He used his cane to point at mistakes and laughed when they finally found one he had made himself.

One of the returned stoneworkers asked for his place at the sluice back. Samon told him the work was there, but the old hours were not. The man had lost sleep under the Receiver and startled whenever a hammer struck stone behind him. For the first week he would work in the open yard with a partner who could trade tasks when noise became too much. He disliked the arrangement until the partner admitted he also needed help lifting the heavier gate boards. They worked out a day both could finish. Mariya watched from the terrace and left them to settle it.

Lios brought water to the work crews in the morning by choice and spent the late light playing a game of stone marks with his aunt's grandchildren. The village agreed that no soldier could assign him a labor route again under the old commission. His cup, made from the bottom of the broken jug, stood by the well when he played. He always took it home at dusk.

On a new clay record, Mariya and the household witnesses wrote the five who had died in the annex and its evacuation: Thesia, Pehl, Annet, Orran, Hessah. The dead from the first attack and the ordinary occupation were written on the same tablet with a space between the groups, so no one could mistake the causes or merge them into a single useful total. Pehl's daughter asked for his name to be read again. Mariya did. Thesia's sister brought her children; they remembered the bread Thesia had asked Mariya to give them. Hessah's mother set the repaired water skin beside the tablet while the clay was still soft.

Pehl's daughter had brought the small stone he used to test fired clay. She struck the edge of the new tablet when it was dry enough to sound. The note was clear. She said her father would have called it good work, then asked whether she could make another tablet for people whose names the first could not hold. Mariya said yes, and asked who would help gather them. The girl named two neighbors at once. Grief had given her no special knowledge; she had learned the craft and knew who could carry clay.

The record could not keep any of them alive. It made it harder for a future scribe to call their absence zero.

After the tablet dried, the households carried the woman-with-bowl statue back toward the ancestor terrace. The Telassari soldiers had wrapped it to keep it from breaking when they removed it. One corner of its base had chipped during the summer; the figure still held her bowl. Mariya and two others helped set it above the well, not in exactly the old place, because a new wall brace occupied the stone beneath it. Nerissa poured a small amount of the first water over its feet. Lios asked whether that would bring anyone back. Nerissa said no. He poured the next small cup anyway, because the living still needed ways to remember who had drawn water before them.

Mariya had no report from Veyr. She had seen Tobious cross Salt Gate alive, wounded and intent on a task he understood. The silence after the root failed told her the doors were closed, not how he had fared beyond one. She would not fill that silence with certainty she had not earned. She also would not live as though uncertainty were a command to stop.

Some mornings she missed him most when the work was easy: when she found a sound jar without checking its rim twice, when the sluice opened after the first turn, when she reached for a second piece of bread and remembered he would have argued over which half was larger. She did not want the missing to become her only way of speaking his name. She told Lios how Tobious had once repaired a water channel after insisting for an hour that it was not broken. Lios laughed and asked whether Tobious had admitted it. Mariya said no; the water had made the admission for him.

***

The last protected seed jar waited in the lower granary. Mariya had opened the other during the hungry weeks, first in secret and later with the households' knowledge. Its grain was gone. The jar Tobious's mother had marked with a leaf had been cracked at the threshold and its seed carried away by soldiers on the ninth day of occupation. No shard had returned to become a relic.

The households gathered around the remaining jar with seed bought and borrowed from the neighboring carriers. Mariya brought a fresh clay tag. She scratched a leaf on it, not to pretend the old vessel had been restored, but so they would know which lot had come from the seed they had kept under the bull seal. A second tag named the grain brought by the coastal villages. Each could be planted and judged by how it grew.

The household witnesses disagreed over how much of the last jar to sow on the high terrace. The soil there drained well but had lost a strip of wall to the occupation works. A lower field had deeper earth and a greater chance of flood. Mariya proposed dividing the lot; a farmer who knew the storm channels argued for keeping the old seed together in one place so they could judge it. They went to see both plots before deciding. Samon showed where the first rain would run. They divided the seed only after the people who would tend each field had said what they could manage. Mariya wrote the choice on the new tag. She had once broken a seal at dawn and explained herself weeks later. The grain went into the ground in daylight with the argument still audible around it.

At the terrace, the rain softened the upper earth without flooding it. Samon marked the first water turn from his chair. Nerissa passed seed to the people kneeling in the rows. Mariya began to set the spacing and then stopped when a younger farmer showed her where runoff would gather after a heavy storm. He moved the next row a little higher. She tested the ground with her fingers and agreed. No one needed her to be right before they could plant.

Lios came down the slope with his cup and asked whether he could put seed in a row. His aunt said he could have a short one after helping carry water to the workers. Mariya showed him how deep to press each grain. He made two holes too close together, corrected them himself, and looked up for approval. She told him the row was his to finish.

The rain grew steadier. It darkened the backs of the people bending over the terrace. Mariya could see the bay between them, the same water that would never again carry a road to Tobious. She remembered his hand beside hers on the granary step and the week they had wanted with no counts at all. She could grieve that life and still want the one beginning here. Neither feeling made the other a lie.

She pressed a seed into the opened earth and reached for another.
