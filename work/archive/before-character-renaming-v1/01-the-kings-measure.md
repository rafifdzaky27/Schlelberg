# SCHLELBERG

## Chapter One — The King's Measure

On the ninth day of the occupation, the soldiers came for the seed.

Toby was waiting outside the granary when the gate's shadow reached the last storage jar. He counted seven soldiers, a scribe, and four donkeys carrying empty panniers. A boy followed them with a water jug. He was too young to have worked a harvest after dark, but someone had put a rope around his neck, like the ropes on the two prisoners beside the well.

Toby looked away before the boy could see him.

Beside him, Maria nudged a small board with her foot. Fourteen pebbles lay on it, one for each jar they had inspected. She drew three apart from the others.

“Let me finish before you say anything.”

“If they take these, we'll have nothing to sow.”

“I know.”

She tied her hair back again. Flour marked her temple where she had wiped away sweat at the mill. She was wearing the same shawl she had worn on the night of the attack. One end had burned; she had cut it on a slant to stop it fraying.

“You said three jars.”

“And I'm still trying to get three jars.”

Boots struck the paving. Toby folded his hands behind his back so the soldiers would not see them close.

From the granary yard, Aras fell away in terraces toward the bay. Mud-brick houses stood on stone foundations, divided by narrow stairs and courtyards where figs should have been drying. Below them lay the harvested barley fields. The olives grew higher up, leaning inland from the sea wind.

Smoke still rose from the boatbuilder's house. Toby no longer noticed the smell. People returning from the groves covered their noses when they passed it.

Across the valley, the red cloth of Telassar flew above the old ruler's house. That building had not burned. The invaders had spared its roof, its oil stores, its stables. Of all the promises the citadel had made to Aras, only its walls remained useful. They now sheltered the men who had broken the village gate.

The officer stopped in front of Maria. A bronze corselet covered his chest; its edges rasped against the padded cloth beneath as he breathed. The other soldiers wore thick tunics and carried spears and hide shields. Without waiting for orders, they took their places on either side of the granary door.

Maria bowed just enough.

“Fourteen jars. Eleven for the palace. Three for sowing after the rains.”

The scribe crouched and rested a clay tablet on his knee. From a fold in his clothes he took a pointed stylus.

“Open them,” the officer said.

Toby lifted the lid from the nearest jar. Barley filled it almost to the neck. He had selected those grains himself: whole, heavy, untouched by beetles. Seed from the patch that had remained standing when last season's winds flattened half the slope.

A soldier dipped a wooden measure into it.

Toby watched the rim.

“That's too deep.”

Maria turned her head.

The soldier stopped. “What?”

“Your measure.” Toby pointed to a dark line around the wood. “The rim used to end there. You've added another band.”

The officer took it from the soldier. He turned it once, examined the join, and handed it back.

“That's the palace measure.”

“It isn't the one you brought yesterday.”

“Yesterday we collected oil.”

“We know how much grain is in this store.”

“Good. You'll find it easier to count what's left.”

The stylus moved across the clay. The scribe had not looked inside a single jar.

Toby stepped forward. Maria caught his wrist.

“Those three are seed,” she told the officer. “Take them and next year you'll have to bring food here before you can collect anything.”

The man considered her.

“How many can still plough?”

“Nineteen. Twenty-one if you release the two beside the well.”

“Oxen?”

“Three healthy pairs.”

Toby looked at her. Yesterday there had been four.

“Two jars,” the officer said. “Load the rest.”

“Three.”

He pointed with his whip handle. “Mark those two.”

The scribe pressed lumps of damp clay around the cords securing their lids. He lifted a carved stone from the thong at his neck and stamped each lump. A bull stood in the impressions, its head lowered.

Maria's fingers remained around Toby's wrist. Then she let go.

“Those two are not to be touched.”

The officer was already turning to supervise the loading.

The soldiers poured barley into sacks. Grains scattered across the paving—not many, but enough to make Toby kneel and gather them. He could not leave them to be trodden underfoot.

When he stood, a soldier was dragging the third jar toward the threshold. A leaf had been scratched into the clay beside its handle.

That one was his.

His mother had made the mark before the clay dried, years ago, so they could find their jar among the things held in common. At the end of every harvest, Toby scrubbed its inside by hand and filled it again. It held the seed for his field, and seed entrusted to him by the family whose house had become the soldiers' kitchen.

“Wait.”

The soldier kept dragging.

Toby caught the other handle.

“I said wait.”

“Let go.”

“Take my share of the food. Leave this.”

The man pulled harder. The base struck the threshold. A small, dry sound came from the clay.

Toby did not think of the seven spears. He saw a crack running down beneath the leaf.

He hauled the jar toward him.

The spear shaft struck his wrist.

His grip opened. Before he could catch the handle again, the shield's rim drove into his breastbone and emptied his lungs. He stumbled backward, trying to keep his feet. A boot swept his ankle out from under him.

His cheek hit the paving.

For a moment he could see only barley. One grain had lodged between two stones. An ant walked across it.

Toby pressed his palms against the ground. His right knuckles scraped the stone as he pulled that hand free from beneath him.

A spear point touched the back of his neck.

“Stay down.”

He stopped moving.

At the gate, nine days ago, he had stopped like this. He had been holding a wooden fork then. A man with a shield had come so close that Toby could no longer bring the tines around. He remembered the wood breaking. Someone had fallen beside him. His own hand had searched the wall for an opening, somewhere narrow enough that the spear could not follow.

He still did not know how to get past a shield.

“He's your sluice worker,” Maria said.

Footsteps approached.

“And?” the officer asked.

“Your men came yesterday. They wanted someone who knew the channels beneath the old storehouse. I gave them his name.”

The spear point pressed a little harder.

“Toby. He repairs the water gates every season.”

The officer told the scribe to check another tablet. When the man nodded, the officer crouched beside Toby's head.

“You'll start now.”

He gripped Toby's hair and lifted his face.

“Under the hill. Until the work is done.”

He let go. Toby's cheek struck the stone again.

When the spear was lifted, he did not immediately rise. The soldiers resumed loading. Wood scraped the sides of the jars, and the grain poured into their sacks. He could hear next year's fields leaving the village.

Maria waited until they had moved away before kneeling beside him.

“Can you breathe?”

He nodded.

“Look at me.”

He turned his head. Beyond her shoulder, the people who had been watching were returning to their work. A woman swept spilled barley into a fold of her dress.

“We still have two,” Maria said quietly. “Help me keep them.”

Toby could not answer.

***

Behind the granary, Maria wet the corner of a cloth and cleaned the blood from his lip.

He supported his right wrist with his left hand. The skin had not yet changed colour, but whenever he moved his fingers, pain travelled to his elbow.

“You gave them my name?”

“They're taking workers from every household. I offered someone who actually knew the channels.”

“Without asking me.”

She stopped wiping.

“You were on Saman's roof until dark yesterday. This morning you came before sunrise. I hadn't had a chance.”

“They killed people at the gate. Now I'm supposed to work for them?”

“Right now you're alive because they need the work done.”

She said it without raising her voice. That made it harder to argue.

She returned to his lip, and this time he let her.

In the lower yard, the water boy struggled to lift his jug onto a donkey's back. A soldier helped him, then tugged the rope around his neck to make him follow.

“They'll come back,” Toby said. “When those two jars are empty, they'll come back.”

“That's why we have to sow.”

“So they can take it again?”

Maria folded the bloodied side of the cloth inward.

“I want the people here to keep eating until we find another way.”

Toby studied his right hand. It could lift a sack Maria could not shift alone. He could dig until sunset, reset the stones of a terrace wall, hold an ox frightened by fire.

In front of the granary, none of it had bought him time to strike back.

“If I'd had a sword—”

“He would have taken it from you.”

Toby looked up.

She lowered the cloth into the bowl. Blood clouded the water.

“I saw it, Toby.”

He almost told her to leave. The words were behind his teeth when he noticed the split skin beneath her thumb, and the flour still caught in her hair. She had not eaten since they opened the granary.

He reached into the fold of cloth at his waist and took out half a barley loaf. It had been flattened beneath him when he fell.

He broke it in two.

Maria accepted the smaller piece, inspected it, then exchanged it for the one in his hand.

They ate without speaking.

***

The old storehouse lay beneath the terrace where Aras kept its images of the dead. Steps descended between two walls so old that no one remembered who had built them. The houses above had fallen and been rebuilt three times. The stones below had not shifted even in the earthquake that brought down the western gate, when Toby was still small enough to be carried on someone's back.

A wooden fence usually barred the stairs. Today it was firewood.

Three soldiers were lowering the statue of the woman with the bowl from its place beside the well. They had wrapped her head in cloth to keep it from breaking.

Toby slowed.

As a child, he had been taught to wet the statue's feet with the first water he drew to drink. The old people said the spring remembered a grateful household. He had never decided whether he believed them. But the pale circle where she had stood made him want to put something there.

A guard pushed him between the shoulders with a spear shaft.

“Down.”

The air below was cooler and smelled of lamp oil. Six people from Aras were clearing rubble and sediment from a passage. Toby recognized Saman by his bent back. The older man was fastening a rope around a block of stone.

“What's wrong with your hand?” he asked.

“Someone hit it.”

Saman looked at his lip, then offered him the unfinished knot.

“Use the other one.”

He asked nothing else.

The passage ended in a circular chamber. Water seeped through the eastern wall and stood ankle-deep across the floor. In the centre, the workers had uncovered an opening beneath a stone slab. Another short flight of steps led down into it.

A Telassari overseer waited there with a three-wicked lamp. A leather tube hung at his waist. None of the other soldiers carried one.

“You know the channels?”

Toby nodded.

The man pointed at the water. “Get it out.”

Toby crouched beside the wall. Moss filled the joints, but the lower stones were clean, worn by the flow. He felt along the floor until his fingers found the drain.

A piece of broken pottery had lodged across its mouth. Mud and roots had caught behind it.

He pulled the shard free.

The water began to move. A faint current tugged at his ankles.

“Don't go down yet.”

The overseer already had one foot on the steps.

“Why?”

Toby pointed at a dark line on the wall. “The water stood higher than the opening. That drain's been blocked a long time. There could still be a chamberful below.”

The man waited. Only a moment, but long enough for Toby to feel something he had not felt all morning: his words had made someone stop.

They lifted the mud out in baskets. Saman held a lamp while Toby worked a wooden rod through the channel. His right wrist began to swell. He slowed, then changed his grip so he would not have to bend it.

As the water receded, a line appeared along the lowest step.

At first Toby took it for a joint in the stone.

It was too straight.

The overseer opened his tube and withdrew a thin piece of hide covered in marks. He looked at the steps, checked the hide, then rolled it away before Toby could make out anything more.

“The rest of you. Out.”

Saman glanced at Toby.

“He stays.”

The workers gathered their baskets. Saman's footsteps paused at the entrance, then followed the others up the passage.

Only Toby, the overseer, and a soldier guarding the stairs remained.

“What's down there?” Toby asked.

“Storage.”

“Storehouse doors usually have handles.”

The overseer raised the lamp.

Toby could see it clearly now: a black surface inside a stone frame, without hinges or sockets for a locking bar. It was wide enough for two people abreast. Mud crusted its lower edge, but the clean part did not reflect the lamp.

The overseer pointed to a recess in the frame.

“Clear that.”

Toby used the corner of his clothes. A piece of green metal crossed the recess like a clamp. One end had broken away.

He touched it with the wooden rod.

A humming travelled through the wood into his palm.

He let go. The rod splashed into the water.

“Pick it up.”

“There's something inside.”

The overseer stepped closer. “I told you to clean it.”

Toby glanced at the way out. The soldier on the steps shifted his spear into his right hand.

Toby crouched again.

He drew the packed mud from behind the clamp with his cloth. Grit caught in the scrape across his knuckles. Without thinking, he wiped his hand against the edge of the recess, leaving a smear of blood.

The humming stopped.

The water around his feet went still.

The overseer lifted his head.

A line of light appeared on the black surface, level with Toby's face.

He stumbled backward into the man's legs. The lamp swayed. All three flames stretched toward the opening, although Toby felt no wind against his skin.

Something metallic moved inside the wall.

The overseer seized his shoulder.

“What did you do?”

Toby did not answer. The light was widening.

Beyond it was sky.

Not another room. Not a passage lit with lamps.

Pale sky opened between buildings taller than the hills above Aras. Roads joined their upper reaches without a single pillar beneath them. Something long and silver passed along one, moving so quickly that Toby only managed to follow its path after it had gone.

He could still feel cold water around his ankles. Behind him, the oil lamp hissed.

A man stood on the other side.

Shadow hid part of his face. His clothes fitted closely across his shoulders and arms, without folds or fastenings Toby recognized. Blood escaped between the fingers pressed against his ribs.

The man looked first at the overseer.

Then he found Toby.

“Don't go with him.”

The voice was hoarse, broken by difficult breaths. Toby knew the way it swallowed the ends of words, the effort to speak without letting anyone hear the fear.

He was listening to his own voice.

The overseer's grip tightened.

“Who is that?”

The man beyond the opening reached toward them. Behind him, a section of roadway bowed downward. Only then did Toby understand that the small things falling from it were people.

“Toby. Listen. They aren't looking for stores.”

A crash sounded in the passage behind him. The soldier on the stairs shouted to someone outside the chamber.

The man came close enough for the light to reach his face.

A long scar ran beneath his left eye. His hair was shorter. He looked older than Toby, though Toby could not have said by how many seasons.

“They came here for you.”
