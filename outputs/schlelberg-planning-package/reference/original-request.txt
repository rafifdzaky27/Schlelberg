# GENERAL WORKING PRINCIPLES

Throughout this project, follow these principles.

### 1. Be analytical, not flattering

Do not tell me that something is “interesting,” “powerful,” “compelling,” or “promising” unless you can explain specifically why.

Avoid generic creative-writing praise.

Instead of saying:

> “The protagonist is interesting.”

Explain:

> “The protagonist becomes interesting in Chapter X because their desire for ___ conflicts directly with their fear of ___, creating an internal contradiction that can generate plot.”

Likewise, criticism should be specific.

Instead of:

> “The pacing is weak.”

Explain:

> “Chapters 4–7 repeat essentially the same dramatic function: the protagonist receives information but does not make meaningful decisions. Because the story state barely changes across four chapters, narrative momentum stalls.”

---

### 2. Separate IDEA QUALITY from EXECUTION QUALITY

A badly written scene may contain an excellent narrative idea.

A well-written scene may serve no useful function.

Always distinguish between:

- concept
- execution
- structural function
- emotional function

Do not discard a strong concept merely because my younger self executed it badly.

---

### 3. Search for the original story's identity

Before changing everything, identify what made me want to write this story in the first place.

Look for recurring elements such as:

- character dynamics
- fantasies
- fears
- emotional conflicts
- philosophical questions
- relationship patterns
- mysteries
- images
- locations
- powers
- technologies
- institutions
- social structures
- tragedies
- moral dilemmas
- recurring symbols
- recurring types of scenes

These may reveal the actual narrative DNA of the novel.

---

### 4. Story logic must dominate convenience

Do not allow events to happen simply because the plot requires them.

For every major event, ask:

- Why does this happen?
- Why does it happen now?
- Who caused it?
- What does that character want?
- What information do they have?
- What alternatives could they reasonably choose?
- Why do they choose this action instead?
- What consequence follows?

Prefer **causal storytelling**:

A causes B.

B forces C.

C creates D.

rather than:

A happens.

Then B happens.

Then C happens.

---

### 5. Characters must generate the plot

Major characters should not merely react to events.

Their:

- desires
- fears
- beliefs
- mistakes
- loyalties
- secrets
- relationships
- ambitions
- decisions

should actively create the story.

Whenever possible, major plot developments should emerge from character choices.

---

### 6. Preserve meaningful imperfections

Do not turn every character into an optimally rational person.

Characters can:

- misunderstand things
- lie to themselves
- make bad decisions
- become jealous
- become obsessive
- act emotionally
- behave hypocritically

But their behavior must make psychological sense.

---

### 7. Avoid generic “Hollywood formula” reconstruction

You may use established narrative theory as analytical tools, including:

- three-act structure
- four-act structure
- five-act structure
- sequence approach
- scene-sequel
- dramatic question
- want vs need
- internal/external conflict
- midpoint reversal
- progressive complications
- climax
- denouement
- setup/payoff
- dramatic irony
- character arc theory

But do NOT mechanically force the story into Save the Cat beats or any other template.

Choose the structure that best fits this particular story.
